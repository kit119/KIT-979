Namo tassa bhagavato arahato sammāsambuddhassa

Homage to the Blessed One, the Arahant, the Perfectly Self-Enlightened One.

Subodhālaṅkāro

The Ornament of Elucidation

1. Dosāvabodha-paṭhamapariccheda

1. The First Chapter: On the Understanding of Faults

Ratanattayappaṇāma

Homage to the Triple Gem

1.

1.

Munindavadanambhoja, gabbhasambhavasundarī;

Saraṇaṃ pāṇinaṃ vāṇī, mayhaṃ pīṇayataṃ manaṃ.

The beautiful speech, born from the lotus-mouth of the Lord of Sages, a refuge for living beings—may it delight my mind.

Nimitta

Introduction

2.

2.

Rāma, sammā’dya’laṅkārā, santi santo purātanā;

Tathāpi tu vaḷañjenti, suddhamāgadhikā na te.

O Rāma, though good and ancient ornaments exist today, yet those who are pure Māgadhī speakers do not employ them.

Abhidhānādikaṃ

Lexicons and so forth.

3.

3.

Tenā’pi nāma toseyya, mete laṅkāravajjite;

Anurūpenā’laṅkāre, ne’sa meso parissamo.

Therefore, even if those [works] devoid of adornment might please me, this my effort is not without a suitable adornment.

4.

4.

Yesaṃ na sañcitā paññā, nekasatthantaro’citā;

Sammoha’bbhāhatā ve’te, nāvabujjhanti kiñcipi.

Those for whom wisdom is not amassed, nor learning from various sciences accumulated—they, truly overwhelmed by delusion, understand nothing at all.

5.

5.

Kiṃ tehi pādasussūsā, yesaṃ natthi garūni’ha;

Ye tappādarajokiṇṇā, te’va sādhū vivekino.

What is the use of attending to the feet of those who have no teachers here? Those who are covered with the dust of their [teachers'] feet, they alone are the good, the discerning.

6.

6.

Kabba, nāṭakanikkhitta, nettacittā kavijjanā;

Yaṃkiñci racayante’taṃ, na vimhayakaraṃ paraṃ.

Poets, whose eyes and minds are fixed on poetry and drama—whatever they compose is not exceedingly wonderful.

7.

7.

Teye’va paṭibhāvento, so’va bandho savimhayo;

Yena tosenti viññū ye, tattha pya’vihitā’darā.

That composition alone is marvelous whereby those [poets], manifesting their genius, satisfy the wise, who in turn hold it in high regard.

8.

8.

Bandho [M 0.0156] ca nāma sadda,tthā, sahitā dosavajjitā;

Pajja gajja vimissānaṃ, bhedenā’yaṃ tidhā bhave.

Composition, by name, is sound and meaning, combined and faultless; by the division of verse, prose, and mixed, it is threefold.

9.

9.

Nibandho cā’nibandho ca, puna dvidhā niruppate;

Taṃ tu pāpentya’laṅkārā, vindanīyatarattanaṃ.

Again, it is defined twofold as structured and unstructured; but ornaments cause that [composition] to attain a state of greater enjoyability.

10.

10.

Anavajjaṃ mukhambhoja “ambhoja”nti padaṃ pāḷiyaṃ natthi, vārijavācakaṃ, sakkaṭaganthato anītaṃ, manavajjā ca bhāratī;

Alaṅkatā’va sobhante, kiṃ nu te nira’laṅkatā?

A faultless lotus-mouth and faultless speech shine only when adorned; what indeed are they, if unadorned?

11.

11.

Vinā garūpadesaṃ taṃ, bālo’laṅkattu micchati;

Sampāpuṇe na viññūhi, hassabhāvaṃ kathaṃ nu so?

Without a teacher's instruction, a fool wishes to create adornment; how could he not attain a state of ridicule from the wise?

12.

12.

Ganthopi kavivācāna, malaṅkāra’ppakāsako;

Yāti tabbacanīyattaṃ, ta’bbohārū’pacārato.

A text, too, which illuminates the ornaments of a poet's speech, comes to be called by that name through usage and metaphorical application.

13.

13.

Dvippakārā alaṅkārā, tattha sadda, tthabhedato;

Saddatthā bandhanāmā’va, taṃsajjita tadāvali.

Ornaments are of two kinds, distinguished by sound and meaning. Sound and meaning are the very components of composition, and the series [of ornaments] is arranged accordingly.

14.

14.

Guṇālaṅkārasaṃyuttā, api dosalava’ṅkitā;

Pasaṃsiyā na viññūhi, sā kaññā viya tādisī.

[A composition], though endowed with virtues and ornaments, if marked by a speck of a fault, is not praised by the wise, just like such a maiden.

15.

15.

Tena dosanirāso’va, mahussāhena sādhiyo;

Niddosā sabbathā sā’yaṃ, saguṇā na bhaveyya kiṃ?

Therefore, the removal of faults must be accomplished with great effort. If this [composition] were entirely faultless, why would it not also be virtuous?

16.

16.

Sā’laṅkāraviyuttā’pi, guṇayuttā manoharā;

Niddosā dosarahitā, guṇayuttā vadhū viya.

Though destitute of ornaments, [a composition] endowed with virtues is charming; faultless and free from flaws, like a virtuous bride.

17.

17.

Pade vākye tadatthe ca, dosā ye vividhā matā;

So’dāharaṇa metesaṃ, lakkhaṇaṃ kathayāmya’haṃ.

The various faults that are recognized in a word, a sentence, and in their meaning—I shall now state the characteristics of these with examples.

Padadosa uddesa

Faults of a Word: Enumeration

18.

18.

Viruddhatthantarā, jhattha, kiliṭṭhāni, virodhi ca;

Neyyaṃ, visesanāpekkhaṃ, hīnatthaka manatthakaṃ.

Having a contradictory secondary meaning, the coarse, the obscure, and the contradictory; the far-fetched, that which requires a qualifier, the inferior in meaning, and the meaningless.

Vākyadosa uddesa

Faults of a Sentence: Enumeration

19.

19.

Dosā padāna vākyāna, mekatthaṃ bhaggarītikaṃ;

Tathā byākiṇṇa gāmmāni, yatihīnaṃ kamaccutaṃ;

Ativutta mapetatthaṃ, sabandhapharusaṃ tathā.

The faults of words and sentences are: tautology, broken style, confusion, vulgarity, defective caesura, faulty sequence, redundancy, meaninglessness, and harshness of composition.

Vākyatthadosauddesa

Faults of Sentence-Meaning: Enumeration

20.

20.

Apakkamo’[M 0.0157], cityahīnaṃ, bhaggarīti, sasaṃsayaṃ;

Gāmmaṃ duṭṭhālaṅkatīti, dosā vākyatthanissitā.

Lack of sequence, lack of propriety, broken style, ambiguity, vulgarity, and faulty adornment—these are the faults related to the meaning of a sentence.

Padadosaniddesa

Explanation of the Faults of a Word

21.

21.

Viruddhatthantaraṃ tañhi, yassa’ññattho virujjhati;

Adhippete yathā megho, visado sukhaye janaṃ.

That indeed is 'contradictory in its other meaning,' wherein another meaning conflicts with the intended one; as for example: 'A clear cloud gladdens the people.'

22.

22.

Visesya madhikaṃ yenā, jhattha metaṃ bhave yathā;

Obhāsitā’sesadiso, khajjoto’yaṃ virājate.

That by which the subject is made excessive is 'abrupt,' as for example: 'Having illuminated all directions, this firefly shines forth.'

23.

23.

Yassa’tthā’vagamo dukkho, pakatyā’divibhāgato;

Kiliṭṭhaṃ taṃ yathā tāya, so’ya māliṅgyate piyā.

That whose meaning is difficult to grasp, due to the analysis of its natural form and so forth, is 'obscure,' as for example: 'By her, O dear one, he is embraced.'

24.

24.

Yaṃ kiliṭṭhapadaṃ mandā, bhidheyyaṃ yamakādikaṃ;

Kiliṭṭhapadadose’va, tampi anto karīyati.

Whatever is an obscure phrase, difficult for the dull-witted to analyze, such as yamaka and so forth, that too is included within the fault of obscure words.

25.

25.

Patītasaddaracitaṃ, siliṭṭhapadasandhikaṃ;

Pasādaguṇasaṃyuttaṃ, yamakaṃ mata medisaṃ.

Composed with pleasing words, with smoothly joined phrases, and endowed with the quality of clarity—such a yamaka is considered to be.

26.

26.

Abyapetaṃ byapeta’ñña, māvuttā’nekavaṇṇajaṃ;

Yamakaṃ tañca pādāna, mādi, majjha, nta, gocaraṃ.

Yamaka is contiguous or non-contiguous, arising from the repetition of various syllables; and it pertains to the beginning, middle, and end of the verse-lines.

Abyapeta paṭhamapādādi yamakaṃ

Contiguous yamaka at the beginning of the first verse-line.

27.

27.

Sujanā’sujanā sabbe, guṇenāpi vivekino;

Vivekaṃ na samāyanti, avivekijanantike.

Good people and bad people, all, even those discerning by their virtue, do not attain discernment when in the presence of undiscerning people.

Abyapeta paṭhama dutiya pādādi yamakaṃ

Contiguous yamaka at the beginning of the first and second verse-lines.

28.

28.

Kusalā’kusalā sabbe, pabalā’pabalā thavā;

No yātā yāva’hosittaṃ, sukhadukkhappadā siyuṃ;

Abyapeta paṭhama dutiya tatiyapādādi yamakaṃ.

All skillful and unskillful, strong or weak, so long as they have not passed into non-existence, may be givers of happiness and suffering. This is a contiguous yamaka at the beginning of the first, second, and third verse-lines.

29.

29.

Sādaraṃ sā daraṃ hantu, vihitā vihitā mayā;

Vandanā vandanāmāna, bhājane ratanattaye.

Respectfully, may she—this salutation—destroy fear; performed, indeed performed by me, is this homage of veneration to the Triple Gem, the receptacle.

Abyapeta catukkapādādi yamakaṃ

Contiguous yamaka at the beginning of all four verse-lines.

30.

30.

Kamalaṃ ka’malaṃ kattuṃ, vanado vanado’mbaraṃ;

Sugato sugato lokaṃ, sahitaṃ sa hitaṃ karaṃ.

To make the lotus pure in the water; the cloud, giver of water, to the sky; the Well-Gone One, having gone well, to the world; He, with them, acting for their welfare.

31.

31.

Abyapetādiyamaka[M 0.0158], sseso leso nidassito;

Ñeyyāni’māyeva disā, ya’ññāni yamakānipi.

Of the yamakas beginning with the contiguous type, this is a brief demonstration. In this same manner, other yamakas are also to be understood.

32.

32.

Accantabahavo tesaṃ, bhedā sambhedayoniyo;

Tathāpi keci sukarā, keci accantadukkarā.

Exceedingly many are their varieties and sources of combination; even so, some are easy, while others are extremely difficult.

33.

33.

Yamakaṃ taṃ pahelī paheḷi (ka.) ca, nekantamadhurāni’ti;

Upekkhiyanti sabbāni, sissakhedabhayā mayā.

That yamaka and the riddle are not entirely pleasant; thus, all of them are overlooked by me from fear of the students' weariness.

34.

34.

Desakālakalāloka, ñāyāgamavirodhi yaṃ;

Taṃ virodhipadaṃ ce’ta, mudāharaṇato phuṭaṃ.

Whatever contradicts place, time, art, worldly convention, logic, or scripture, that is a 'contradictory phrase'; and this is clear from the examples.

35.

35.

Ya dappatīta mānīya, vattabbaṃ neyya māhu taṃ;

Yathā sabbāpi dhavalā, disā rocanti rattiyaṃ.

That which is not evident and must be stated by means of inference, they call 'to be inferred' (neyya); as for example: 'All the white directions shine at night.'

36.

36.

Nedisaṃ bahu maññanti, sabbe sabbattha viññuno;

Dullabhā’vagatī sadda, sāmatthiyavilaṅghinī.

Wise people everywhere do not hold such a thing in high esteem, for understanding that transgresses the capacity of the words is difficult to attain.

37.

37.

Siyā visesanāpekkhaṃ, yaṃ taṃ patvā visesanaṃ;

Sātthakaṃ taṃ yathā taṃ so, bhiyyo passati cakkhunā.

That which requires a qualifier, and becomes meaningful only after obtaining that qualifier, is 'dependent'; as for example: 'He sees it more with the eye.'

38.

38.

Hīnaṃ kare visesyaṃ yaṃ, taṃ hīnatthaṃ bhave yathā;

Nippabhī kata khajjoto, samudeti divākaro.

Whatever renders the subject inferior is 'of inferior meaning,' as for example: 'The sun, having rendered the firefly lustreless, arises.'

39.

39.

Pādapūraṇamattaṃ yaṃ, anatthamiti taṃ mataṃ;

Yathā hi vande buddhassa, pādapaṅkeruhaṃ pi ca.

Whatever is merely for filling a verse-line is considered meaningless; as for example: 'For (hi) I revere the lotus-feet of the Buddha, and (ca) also (pi).'

Vākyadosa niddesa

Exposition of Faults in the Sentence

40.

40.

Saddato atthato vuttaṃ, yattha bhiyyopi vuccati;

Ta mekatthaṃ yathā’bhāti, vārido vārido ayaṃ.

Where that which has been stated by word and by meaning is stated yet again, that is 'tautological'; as for example: 'This cloud, this water-giver, shines.'

Yathā ca

And as another example:

41.

41.

Titthiyaṅkurabījāni, jahaṃ diṭṭhigatāni’ha;

Pasādeti pasanne’so, mahāmuni mahājane.

Abandoning here wrong views, the seeds of heretical sprouts, this Great Sage inspires faith in the faithful among the great multitude.

42.

42.

Āraddhakkamavicchedā, bhaggarīti bhave yathā;

Kāpi paññā, kopi paguṇo, pakatīpi aho tava.

From the interruption of the established sequence comes 'broken rhythm,' as for example: 'Some wisdom, some skill, and oh, your nature too!'

43.

43.

Padānaṃ [M 0.0159] dubbinikkhepā, byāmoho yattha jāyati;

Taṃ byākiṇṇanti viññeyyaṃ, tadudāharaṇaṃ yathā.

Where confusion arises from the poor arrangement of words, that should be understood as 'disordered'; its example is as follows.

44.

44.

Bahuguṇe paṇamati, dujjanānaṃ pyayaṃ jano;

Hitaṃ pamudito niccaṃ, sugataṃ samanussaraṃ.

This person bows to him of many virtues, and also to the wicked; always delighting in what is beneficial, while recollecting the Well-Gone One.

45.

45.

Visiṭṭhavacanā’petaṃ, gāmmaṃ’tya’bhimataṃ yathā;

Kaññe kāmayamānaṃ maṃ, na kāmayasi kiṃnvi’daṃ?

That which is devoid of refined speech is considered 'rustic,' as for example: 'O maiden, why do you not desire me, who am desiring you? What indeed is this?'

46.

46.

Padasandhānato kiñci, duppatītikaraṃ bhave;

Tampi gāmmaṃ tya’bhimataṃ, yathā yābhavato piyā.

Something difficult to understand due to word-connection; that too is considered rustic, as in the example: 'whatever is beloved to you.'

47.

47.

Vuttesu sūcite ṭṭhāne, padacchedo bhave yati;

Yaṃ tāya hīnaṃ taṃ vuttaṃ, yatihīnanti sā pana.

In stated or indicated places, a word-break, which is a pause, may occur; that which is deficient in this is said to be 'lacking a pause.'

48.

48.

Yati sabbatthapādante, vuttaḍḍhe ca visesato;

Pubbāparānekavaṇṇa, padamajjhepi katthaci.

A pause occurs at the end of every line, and especially in a half-verse; and sometimes also in the middle of a word that has many syllables preceding and following.

Tatthodāharaṇapaccudāharaṇāni yathā

In this regard, the examples and counter-examples are as follows:

49.

49.

Taṃ name sirasā cāmi, karavaṇṇaṃ tathāgataṃ;

Sakalāpi disā siñca, tiva soṇṇarasehi yo.

I bow my head to him, the Tathāgata of golden hue, who, as it were, sprinkles all directions with golden nectar.

50.

50.

Saro sandhimhi pubbanto, viya lope vibhattiyā;

Aññathā tva’ññathā tattha, yā’desādi parā’di’va.

A vowel at the end of a preceding word in sandhi is like the elision of a case-ending; otherwise, it is different, as in the case of substitution and so forth, which are like suffixes and so forth.

51.

51.

Cādī pubbapadantā’va, niccaṃ pubbapadassitā;

Pādayo niccasambandhā, parādīva parena tu.

Particles such as 'ca' are always at the end of the preceding word and always dependent on it; whereas particles such as 'pa' are always connected with the word that follows, like suffixes.

Sabbatthodāharaṇāni yathā

Examples for all cases are as follows:

52.

52.

Name taṃ sirasā sabbo, pamā’tītaṃ tathāgataṃ;

Yassa lokaggataṃ patta, sso’pamā na hi yujjati.

All bow their heads to that Tathāgata, who is beyond measure; for him who has attained the summit of the world, no comparison is fitting.

53.

53.

Munindaṃ taṃ sadā vandā, mya’nantamati muttamaṃ;

Yassa paññā ca mettā ca, nissīmāti vijambhati.

I always venerate that Sage-Lord, the supreme one of boundless intellect, whose wisdom and loving-kindness shine forth as being without limit.

Cādipādīsu paccudāharaṇāni yathā

Counter-examples concerning particles such as 'ca' and 'pa' are as follows:

54.

54.

Mahāmettā mahāpaññā, ca yattha paramodayā;

Paṇamāmi jinaṃ taṃ pa, varaṃ varaguṇā’layaṃ.

Great loving-kindness, great wisdom, and where there is ultimate prosperity; I bow to that Victor, pa, the excellent one, the abode of excellent qualities.

55.

55.

Padatthakkamato muttaṃ, kamaccuta midaṃ yathā;

Khettaṃ vā dehi gāmaṃ vā, desaṃ vā mama sobhanaṃ.

That which is devoid of the proper sequence of words is 'fallen from order,' as for example: 'Give me a field, or a village, or a country, my lovely one.'

56.

56.

Lokiyattha [M 0.0160] matikkantaṃ, ativuttaṃ mataṃ yathā;

Atisambādha mākāsa, metissā thanajambhane.

That which transcends worldly sense is considered 'overstated,' as for example: 'The sky is too constricted by the swelling of her breasts.'

57.

57.

Samudāyatthato’petaṃ, taṃ apetatthakaṃ yathā;

Gāviputto balibaddho, tiṇaṃ khādī pivī jalaṃ.

That which is devoid of collective meaning is 'devoid of meaning,' as for example: 'A cow's son, a bull, ate grass and drank water.'

58.

58.

Bandhe pharusatā yattha, taṃ bandhapharusaṃ yathā;

Kharā khilā parikkhīṇā, khette khittaṃ phalatya’laṃ.

Where there is harshness in the composition, that is 'harsh composition,' as for example: 'Harsh, barren, and exhausted, the seed sown in the field yet yields abundant fruit.'

Vākyatthadosa niddesa

Exposition of Faults in the Meaning of a Sentence

59.

59.

Ñeyyaṃ lakkhaṇa manvattha, vasenā’pakkamādinaṃ;

Udāharaṇa metesaṃ, dāni sandassayāmya’haṃ.

The characteristics of 'deviation' and so forth are to be known according to the literal meaning of the terms; now I shall present examples of these.

Tatthā’pakkamaṃ yathā

In this regard, 'deviation' is as follows:

60.

60.

Bhāvanā, dāna, sīlāni, sammā sampāditāni’ha;

Bhoga, saggādi, nibbāna, sādhanāni na saṃsayo.

Meditation, generosity, and virtue, when rightly perfected here, are without a doubt the means for accomplishing wealth, heaven and so forth, and Nibbāna.

Ocityahīnaṃ yathā

'Lacking propriety' is as follows:

61.

61.

Pūjanīyataro loke, aha meko nirantaraṃ;

Mayekasmiṃ guṇā sabbe, yato samuditā ahuṃ.

I alone in the world am ever more worthy of veneration, for in me alone have all virtues arisen.

Yathā ca

And as another example:

62.

62.

Yācito’haṃ kathaṃ nāma, na dajjāmya’pi jīvitaṃ;

Tathāpi puttadānena, vedhate hadayaṃ mama.

Being asked, how could I not give even my own life? Nevertheless, at the giving of my son, my heart trembles.

Bhaggarīti yathā

'Broken style' is as follows:

63.

63.

Itthīnaṃ dujjanānañca, vissāso nopapajjate;

Vise siṅgimhi nadiyaṃ, roge rājakulamhi ca.

Trust should not be placed in women and wicked people; nor in poison, a horned animal, a river, sickness, or a royal family.

Sasaṃsayaṃ yathā

'Doubtful' is as follows:

64.

64.

Munindacandimā loka, saralolavilocano;

Jano’ vakkantapantho’va, gopadassanapīṇito.

The Sage-Lord is the moon, the eye of the world, with clear and moving glances; the people, like travelers on a deserted path, are gladdened by the sight of a cow's footprint.

65.

65.

Vākyatthato duppatīti, karaṃ gāmmaṃ mataṃ yathā;

Poso vīriyavā so’yaṃ, paraṃ hantvā na vissamī.

That which is difficult to understand from the meaning of the sentence is considered 'rustic,' as for example: 'This vigorous man, having slain the enemy, I do not rest.'

66.

66.

Duṭṭhālaṅkaraṇaṃ tetaṃ tvethaṃ (?), yatthā’laṅkāradūsanaṃ;

Tassā’laṅkāraniddese, rūpa māvi bhavissati.

That is a 'flawed adornment' where there is a corruption of the figure of speech itself. Its nature will become manifest in the exposition on figures of speech.

67.

67.

Kato’tra [M 0.0161] saṅkhepanayā mayā’yaṃ,

Dosāna mesaṃ pavaro vibhāgo;

Eso’va’laṃ bodhayituṃ kavīnaṃ,

Tamatthi ce khedakaraṃ parampi.

Here, by me, this supreme classification of these faults has been made in brief; this alone is enough to instruct poets, if there is something further that causes weariness.

Iti saṅgharakkhitamahāsāmiviracite subodhālaṅkāre

Thus, in the Subodhālaṅkāra composed by the great elder Saṅgharakkhita,

2. Dosaparihārāvabodha-dutiyapariccheda

2. The Second Chapter: Understanding the Remedying of Faults

68.

68.

Kadāci kavikosallā, virodho sakalo pya’yaṃ;

Dosasaṅkhya matikkamma, guṇavīthiṃ vigāhate.

Sometimes, by the skill of a poet, this entire contradiction, transcending the category of faults, enters the path of virtues.

69.

69.

Tena vuttavirodhāna, mavirodho yathā siyā;

Tathā dosaparihārā, vabodho dāni nīyate.

Therefore, so that there may be non-contradiction regarding the stated contradictions, the understanding of the remedying of faults is now set forth.

Tattha viruddhatthantarassa parihāro yathā

Therein, the remedying of contradiction with another meaning is as follows:

70.

70.

Vindantaṃ pākasālīnaṃ, sālīnaṃ dassanā sukhaṃ;

Taṃ kathaṃ nāma megho’yaṃ, visado sukhaye janaṃ?

For one who obtains ripe rice, the sight of the rice fields is a pleasure; how then indeed could this clear cloud make people happy?

Yathā vā

Or, for example:

71.

71.

Vināyakopi nāgo si, gotamopi mahāmati;

Paṇītopi rasā’peto, cittā me sāmi te gati.

You are a Leader (Vināyaka), yet also a great being (nāga); though a Gotama, you are of great wisdom; though sublime (paṇīta), you are devoid of passion (rasa); O Lord, wondrous to me is your way.

Ajha’tthassa yathā

As for internal contradiction:

72.

72.

Kathaṃ tādiguṇābhāve, lokaṃ toseti dujjano?

Obhāsitāsesadiso, khajjoto nāma kiṃ bhave?

In the absence of such qualities, how can a wicked person please the world? What indeed would a firefly be, having illuminated all directions?

That which even the assembled wise perceive as the 'essence of binding,' having reached the opportunity for insight, is this quality named 'concentration' (samādhi).

150.

Sammā ādhīyate’cce’so, ‘samādhī’ti niruccati.

When a property of one thing is rightly established upon another, in keeping with the limits of convention, this is therefore called 'samādhi'.

4. Atthālaṅkārāvabodha-catutthapariccheda

4. The Fourth Chapter: The Understanding of the Ornaments of Meaning

164.

164.

Atthālaṅkārasahitā, saguṇā bandhapaddhati;

Accantakantā kantā yato accantakantā (ka.) va vuccante te tato’dhunā.

A style of composition, accompanied by ornaments of meaning and possessing good qualities, is exceedingly lovely, like a beloved woman; therefore, they are now explained.

165.

165.

Sabhāva, vaṅkavuttīnaṃ, bhedā dvidhā alaṃkriyā;

Paṭhamā tattha vatthūnaṃ, nānāvatthā’vibhāvinī.

Ornamentation is twofold by division: natural expression and indirect expression. The first among them reveals the various states of things.

Yathā

For example:

166.

166.

Līlā vikanti subhago, disā thira vilokano;

Bodhisattaṅkuro bhāsaṃ, viroci vāca māsabhiṃ.

Playful, graceful, and handsome, gazing steadily in all directions, the Bodhisatta-sprout shone forth with radiance and uttered a noble speech.

167.

167.

Tassā’nantavikappattā, hoti bījo’padassanaṃ.

The other mode of expression is that which is otherwise than the inherent nature of a thing; on account of its endless variations, only an indication of its seed is given.

Therein are: hyperbole, simile, metaphor, natural description, illuminator; objection, corroboration, contrast, peculiar cause.

Niddesa

Exposition

173.

173.

Pakāsakā visesassa, siyā’tisayavutti yā;

Lokā’tikkantavisayā, lokiyā,ti ca sā dvidhā.

The mode of expression that reveals a special quality is hyperbole. It is twofold: having a world-transcending subject, and worldly.

174.

174.

Lokiyātisayasse’te,

Bhedā ye jātiādayo;

Paṭipādīyate tva’jja, lokātikkantagocarā.

The divisions of worldly hyperbole, such as those of genus and so on, are known; but today, that which pertains to the world-transcending sphere is explained.

175.

175.

Pivanti dehakantī ye, nettañjalipuṭena te;

Nā’laṃ hantuṃ jine’saṃ tvaṃ, taṇhaṃ taṇhāharopi kiṃ?

Those who drink his bodily beauty with the cupped hands of their eyes—you, O Craving, are not able to destroy the Conqueror; how could you, when he is the very remover of craving?

176.

176.

Upamāno’pameyyānaṃ, sadhammattaṃ siyo’pamā;

Sadda, tthagammā, vākyattha, visayā,ti ca sā bhidhā.

A simile is the possession of a common property by the standard of comparison and the object of comparison. And it is threefold: word-based, meaning-based, and sentence-meaning-based.

177.

177.

Samāsa, paccaye, vā’dī, saddā tesaṃ vasā tidhā;

Saddagammā samāsena, munindo candimā’nano.

The word-based simile is threefold, by means of: compound, affix, and particles like 'vā'. By compound: 'the Lord of Sages, with a face like the moon'.

178.

178.

Āyādī paccayā tehi, vadanaṃ paṅkajāyate;

Munindanayana dvandaṃ, nīluppaladalīyati.

By affixes like '-āya' and so on: 'the face becomes like a lotus'; 'the pair of eyes of the Lord of Sages becomes like blue lotus petals'.

179.

179.

Ivādī iva, vā, tulya, samāna, nibha, sannibhā;

Yathā, saṅkāsa, tulita, ppakāsa, patirūpakā.

Particles like 'iva' and so on: iva, vā, tulya, samāna, nibha, sannibha; yathā, saṅkāsa, tulita, pakāsa, patirūpaka.

180.

180.

Sarī, sarikkha, saṃvādī, virodhi, sadisā, viya;

Paṭipakkha, paccanīkā, sapakkho, pamito, pamā.

Sarī, sarikkha, saṃvādī, virodhi, sadisa, viya; paṭipakkha, paccanīka, sapakkha, pamita, pamā.

181.

181.

Paṭibimba, paṭicchanna, sarūpa, sama, samitā;

Savaṇṇā, bhā, paṭinidhi, sadhammā, di salakkhaṇā.

Paṭibimba (reflection), paṭicchanna (concealed), sarūpa (likeness), sama (same), samita (equal); savaṇṇa (of the same kind), bhā (luster), paṭinidhi (substitute), sadhamma (having the same quality), and so on are its characteristics.

182.

182.

Jayatya[M 0.0172], kkosati, hasati, patigajjati, dūbhati;

Usūyatya, vajānāti, nindati, ssati, rundhati.

He excels, scolds, laughs at, rivals, harms; he envies, disparages, blames, is jealous of, obstructs.

183.

183.

Tassa coreti sobhaggaṃ, tassa kantiṃ vilumpati;

Tena saddhiṃ vivadati, tulyaṃ tenā’dhirohati.

He steals its charm, he plunders its beauty; he disputes with it, he rises to equality with it.

184.

184.

Kacchaṃ vigāhate, tassa, ta manvetya, nubandhati;

Taṃsīlaṃ, taṃnisedheti, tassa cā’nukaroti, me.

He rivals it, he follows it, he pursues it; he shares its nature, he opposes it, and he imitates it.

185.

185.

Upamāno’pameyyānaṃ, sadhammattaṃ vibhāvibhi;

Imehi upamābhedā, keci niyyanti sampati.

By these who discern the common quality of the standard of comparison and the object of comparison, some divisions of similes are now set forth.

186.

186.

Vikāsipadumaṃ’vā’ti, sundaraṃ sugatā’nanaṃ;

Iti dhammopamā nāma, tulyadhammanidassanā.

'Like a blooming lotus,' thus, 'fair is the face of the Well-Gone One.' This is called the simile of quality, a showing of equivalent qualities.

187.

187.

Dhammahīnā “mukha’mbhoja, sadisaṃ munino”iti;

Viparīto’pamā “tulya, mānanena’mbujaṃ tava”.

The simile devoid of a common quality: 'The face of the sage is like a lotus.' The inverted simile: 'Your lotus is equal to your face.'

188.

188.

Tavā’nana’miva’mbhojaṃ, ambhoja’miva te mukhaṃ;

Aññamaññopamā sā’yaṃ, aññamaññopamānato.

'A lotus is like your face; your face is like a lotus.' This is the mutual simile, from mutual comparison.

189.

189.

“Yadi kiñci bhave’mbhojaṃ, locana’bbhamuvibbhamaṃ;

Dhāretuṃ mukhasobhaṃ taṃ, tave”ti abbhutopamā.

'If there were any lotus, with the roving beauty of your eyes and brow, to bear that splendor of your face'—this is the astounding simile.

190.

190.

“Sugandhi sobhā sambandhī, sisiraṃ’su virodhi ca;

Mukhaṃ tava’mbujaṃve’ti”, sā silesopamā matā.

'Fragrant, endowed with beauty, cool, and opposing the sun—your face is indeed a lotus.' This is considered the pun-simile.

191.

191.

Sarūpasaddavāccattā, sā santānopamā yathā;

Bālā’vu’yyānamālā’yaṃ, sā’lakā’nanasobhinī.

Because it is expressed by words of similar form, it is the continuous simile, for instance: 'This young girl, a garden garland, shines with locks of hair upon her face.'

192.

192.

Khayī cando, bahurajaṃ, padumaṃ, tehi te mukhaṃ;

Samānampi samukkaṃsi, tya’yaṃ nindopamā matā.

The moon wanes, the lotus has much pollen; by these, your face, though equal, surpasses. This is considered the blame-simile.

193.

193.

Asamattho mukheni’ndu, jina! Te paṭigajjituṃ;

Jaḷo kalaṅkī’ti ayaṃ, paṭisedhopamā siyā.

'The moon is unable, O Conqueror, to rival your face; it is dull and blemished'—this would be the refutation-simile.

194.

194.

“Kacchaṃ candāravindānaṃ, atikkamma mukhaṃ tava;

Attanā’va samaṃ jāta”, mitya’sādhāraṇopamā.

'Surpassing a thicket of moons and lotuses, your face is born equal to itself'—this is the incomparable simile.

195.

195.

“Sabba’mbhoja’ppabhāsāro, rāsibhūto’va katthaci;

Tavā’nanaṃ vibhātī”ti, hotā’bhūtopamā ayaṃ.

'Your face shines forth as if the very essence of the splendor of all lotuses were gathered into a heap somewhere'—this is the unreal simile.

196.

196.

Patīyate’tthagammā tu, saddasāmatthiyā kvaci;

Samāsa, ppaccaye, vādi, saddayogaṃ vinā api.

The meaning-based simile, however, is understood sometimes by the inherent power of the word, even without the use of words such as compounds, suffixes, or 'iva'.

197.

197.

Bhiṅgāne’māni [M 0.0173] cakkhūni, nā’mbujaṃ mukha’mevi’daṃ;

Subyattasadisattena, sā sarūpopamā matā.

'These eyes are bees, and this face is indeed a lotus.' By its clearly expressed likeness, this is considered the simile of identical form.

198.

198.

“Maye’va mukhasobhā’sse, tyala’mindu! Vikatthanā;

Yato’mbujepi sā’tthīti”, parikappopamā ayaṃ.

'If splendor of face were in me alone, then enough, O moon, with your boasting! For it exists in the lotus as well'—this is the supposition-simile.

199.

199.

“Kiṃ vā’mbuja’ntobhantāli, kiṃ lolanayanaṃ mukhaṃ;

Mama dolāyate citta”, micca’yaṃ saṃsayopamā.

'Is it a lotus with bees wandering within, or is it a face with restless eyes? My mind wavers'—this is the doubt-simile.

200.

200.

Kiñci vatthuṃ padassetvā, sadhammassā’bhidhānato;

Sāmyappatītisabbhāvā, pativatthupamā yathā.

Having shown some object, by stating a common quality, where the understanding of similarity exists—that is the counterpart simile, as for example:

201.

201.

Janesu jāyamānesu, ne’kopi jinasādiso;

Dutiyo nanu natthe’va, pārijātassa pādapo.

Among beings who are born, not even one is like the Conqueror; surely, a second Pārijāta tree does not exist.

202.

202.

Vākyatthene’va vākyattho, yadi kocū’pamīyate;

Ivayuttā, viyuttattā, sā vākyatthopamā dvidhā.

If one sentence-meaning is compared by another sentence-meaning, that sentence-meaning simile is twofold: connected with 'iva' and unconnected with it.

Ivayuttā

Connected with 'iva'

203.

203.

Jino saṃklesatattānaṃ, āvibhūto janāna’yaṃ;

Ghammasantāpatattānaṃ, ghammakāle’mbudo viya.

This Conqueror, manifest to people afflicted by defilements, is like a rain cloud in the hot season to those afflicted by the heat.

Ivaviyuttā

Unconnected with 'iva'

204.

204.

Munindānana mābhāti, vilāsekamanoharaṃ;

Uddhaṃ samuggatassā’pi, kiṃ te canda vijambhanā.

The face of the Lord of Sages shines, singularly captivating in its grace; though you have risen high, O moon, what is this boasting of yours?

205.

205.

Samubbejeti dhīmantaṃ, bhinnaliṅgādikaṃ tu yaṃ;

Upamādūsanāyā’la, metaṃ katthaci taṃ yathā.

Whatever is of different gender and so on, it agitates the wise; this is sufficient to spoil a simile in some cases. For instance:

206.

206.

Haṃsī’vā’yaṃ sasī bhinna, liṅgā, kāsaṃ sarāni’va;

Vijāti vacanā, hīnā, sā’va bhatto bhaṭo’dhipe.

'This moon is like a female swan'—(this is faulty due to) different gender. 'Arrows are like kāsa-grass'—(this is faulty due to) different class and number. An inferior comparison is also a fault.

207.

207.

“Khajjoto bhāṇumālī’va, vibhāti”tyadhikopamā;

Aphuṭṭhatthā “balambodhi, sāgaro viya saṃkhubhi.”

'A firefly shines like the sun'—this is the excessive simile. With an unconnected meaning: 'The ocean of the army was agitated like the ocean.'

208.

208.

“Cande kalaṅko bhiṅgo’ve’, tyu’pamāpekkhinī ayaṃ;

Khaṇḍitā keravā’kāro, sakalaṅko nisākaro.

'The stain on the moon is like a bee'—this is the dependent simile. It is incomplete; the night-maker is stained and has the appearance of a white water-lily.

209.

209.

Iccevamādirūpesu, bhavanti vigatā’darā;

Karonti cā’daraṃ dhīrā, payoge kvaci de’va tu.

In such and similar forms, some are unconcerned; but the wise show concern, though only in some applications.

210.

210.

Itthīyaṃ’vā’jano yāti, vadatye’sā pumā viya;

Piyo pāṇā ivā’yaṃ me, vijjā dhana’miva’ccitā.

This person goes like a woman; she speaks like a man. This one is dear to me like life; knowledge is honored like wealth.

211.

211.

Bhavaṃ [M 0.0174] viya mahīpāla, devarājā virocate;

Ala’maṃsumato kacchaṃ, tejasā rohituṃ ayaṃ.

O king, you shine like the king of gods; this one is able to ascend with splendor to the very realm of the sun.

212.

212.

Upamāno’pameyyānaṃ, abhedassa nirūpanā;

Upamā’va tirobhūta, bhedā rūpaka muccate.

The presentation of non-difference between the standard of comparison and the object of comparison is called a metaphor; it is the simile itself, with its distinction concealed.

213.

213.

Asesa vatthu visayaṃ, ekadesa vivutti vivatti (ṭīkā), ca;

Taṃ dvidhā puna paccekaṃ, samāsādivasā tidhā.

It is of two kinds: pertaining to the entire object, and partial description. Each of these is again threefold by way of compound and so on.

Asesavatthuvisayasamāsa

Compound pertaining to the entire object.

214.

214.

Aṅgulidala saṃsobhiṃ, nakhadīdhiti kesaraṃ;

Sirasā na pilandhanti, ke muninda pada’mbujaṃ.

Adorned with finger-petals and nail-ray filaments, who, O Lord of Sages, would not place upon their head your lotus-foot?

Asesavatthuvisayaasamāsa

Non-compound pertaining to the entire object.

215.

215.

Ratanāni guṇā bhūrī, karuṇā sītalaṃ jalaṃ;

Gambhīratta magādhattaṃ, paccakkho’yaṃ jino’mbudhi.

Abundant virtues are jewels, compassion is cool water, profundity and unfathomableness; this Victor is a manifest ocean.

Asesavatthuvisayamissaka

Mixed metaphor pertaining to the entire object.

216.

216.

Candikā mandahāsā te, muninda! Vadani’nduno;

Pabodhayatya’yaṃ sādhu, mano kumuda kānanaṃ.

O Lord of Sages, your gentle smile is the moonlight from the moon of your face; this well awakens the water-lily grove of the mind.

217.

217.

Asesavatthuvisaye, pabhedo rūpake ayaṃ;

Ekadesavivuttimhi, bhedo dāni pavuccati.

This is a division of metaphor concerning the entire object; now the division concerning partial description is declared.

Ekadesavivuttisamāsa

Compound with partial description.

218.

218.

Vilāsa hāsa kusumaṃ, rucirā’dhara pallavaṃ;

Sukhaṃ ke vā na vindanti, passantā munino mukhaṃ.

The flower of a graceful smile, the charming lip-sprout; who indeed would not find happiness, seeing the face of the Sage?

Ekadesavivuttiasamāsa

Non-compound with partial description.

219.

219.

Pādadvandaṃ munindassa, dadātu vijayaṃ tava;

Nakharaṃsī paraṃ kantā, yassa pāpajayaddhajā.

May the pair of feet of the Lord of Sages grant you victory; whose supremely lovely nail-rays are the banners of the conquest of evil.

Ekadesavivuttimissaka

Mixed metaphor with partial description.

220.

220.

Sunimmalakapolassa, muninda vadani’nduno;

Sādhu’ppabuddha hadayaṃ, jātaṃ kerava kānanaṃ.

Of the Lord of Sages, whose cheeks are spotless, whose face is like the moon; the well-awakened heart has become a water-lily grove.

221.

221.

Rūpakāni [M 0.0175] bahūnye’va ṭīkāyaṃ uddhaṭaṃ yuttarūpakaṃ sitapupphujalaṃ lola, nettabhiṅga tavā’nanaṃ; kassa nāma mano dhīra, nākaḍḍhati manoharaṃ;, yuttā, yuttādibhedato;

Visuṃ na tāni vuttāni, etthe’va’ntogadhāni’ti.

Metaphors are indeed many, differentiated as 'fitted' and so on; they are not stated separately, for they are included herein.

222.

222.

“Candimā’kāsapaduma”, miccetaṃ khaṇḍarūpakaṃ;

Duṭṭha, “mamboruhavanaṃ, nettāni’ccā”di sundaraṃ.

'The moon is a sky-lotus'—this is a defective metaphor; it is faulty. But 'the face a lotus-forest, the eyes...' and so on is beautiful.

223.

Tevā’nubhāvā dhiti,ma, tyā’dayo byabhicārino.

Enthusiasm in matters of majesty, courage, and so forth, is designated as 'heroism'. By association with battle, giving, and compassion, this heroism may be of three kinds. Resolve, intellect, sacrifice, and so forth are its transient states.

Bhayaṭṭhāyībhāva

The Stable State of Fear

364.

364.

Vikārā,sani,sattā’di, bhayu’kkaṃso bhayānako;

Sedā’dayo nubhāve’ttha, tāsā’dī byabhicārino.

From distorted sounds, thunderbolts, fierce creatures, and the like, the intensification of fear is the dreadful rasa. Here, sweat and so forth are the consequents; trembling and the like are the transient states.

Jigucchāṭṭhāyībhāva

The Stable State of Disgust

365.

365.

Jigucchā rudhirā’dīhi, pūtyā’dīhi virāgato;

Bībhaccho khobhanu’bbegī, kamena karuṇāyuto;

Nāsā vikūṇanādīhi, saṅkādīhi’ssa posanaṃ.

Disgust arises from blood and so forth, and from putrid things, due to aversion. The sentiment of disgust is agitating and disturbing, gradually accompanied by compassion. Its nourishment is by wrinkling of the nose and so forth, and by suspicion and so forth.

Vimhayaṭṭhāyībhāva

The Stable State of Astonishment

366.

366.

Ati loka padatthehi, vimhayo’yaṃ raso’bbhuto;

Tassā’nubhāvā seda,ssu, sādhuvādā’dayo siyuṃ;

Tāsā,vega,dhiti,ppaññā, honte’ttha byabhicārino.

From supramundane objects, this wonder is the wondrous rasa. Its consequents may be sweat, tears, exclamations of 'well done', and so forth. Here, trembling, agitation, resolve, and wisdom are the transient states.

Samaṭṭhāyībhāva

The Abiding State of Equanimity

367.

367.

Ṭhāyībhāvo samo mettā, dayā,modā’di sambhavo;

Bhāvādīhi ta’dukkaṃso, santo santa nisevito.

The abiding state is equanimity, arising from loving-kindness, compassion, sympathetic joy, and so forth. Its excellence is achieved through cultivation; it is resorted to by the peaceful.
