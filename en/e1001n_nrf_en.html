Yathādhammikarājūnaṃ, amaccā ca purohitā;

Nītisatthaṃ sunissāya, nicchayanti vinicchayaṃ.

The ministers and priests of righteous kings, relying well on the treatise on policy, determine judgments.

Aṅgāni vedā cattāro, mīmaṃsānyāya vitthāro;

Dhammasatthaṃ purāṇañca, vijjā hetā catuddasa.

The four Vedas with their six auxiliary disciplines, Mīmāṃsā, Nyāya, the Purāṇas, and the Dharmaśāstras—these are the fourteen branches of knowledge.

Āyubbedo manubbedo, gandhabbo ceti te tayo;

Atthasatthaṃ catutthañca, vijjāhyāṭṭharasa matā.

Āyurveda, the science of humanity, and the science of music are three; and the Arthaśāstra is the fourth. Together with the previous ones, these are considered the eighteen branches of knowledge.

Sutisamutisaṅkhyā ca, rogānīti visesakā;

Gandhabbā gaṇikā ceva, dhanubbedā ca pūraṇā.

Knowledge based on hearing, convention, and calculation; the special art of diagnosing disease; the arts of music, courtesanship, archery, and fulfillment.

Tikicchā itihāso ca, jotimāyā ca chandati;

Ketumantā ca saddā ca, sippāṭṭhārasakā ime.

Medicine, history, astrology and magic, prosody; the science of omens and mantras, and the science of sounds—these are the eighteen crafts.

Damo daṇḍo itikhyāto, taṭṭhādaṇḍo mahīpati;

Tassa nīti daṇḍanīti, nayanānīti vuccati.

Restraint is known as 'the rod' (daṇḍa); therefore, the king is the wielder of the rod. His policy is Daṇḍanīti, which is also called Nayanānīti, the policy of guidance.

Daṇḍena nīyate cedaṃ, daṇḍaṃ nayati vā puna;

Daṇḍanīti itikhyāto, tilokā nati vattate.

This world is guided by the rod, and the ruler in turn guides the rod. This is known as Daṇḍanīti; through it, the three worlds proceed in submission.

Nānāsatthoddhataṃ vakkhe, rājanīti samuccayaṃ;

Sabbabījamiduṃ satthaṃ, cāṇakya sārasaṅgahaṃ.

I shall explain this collection of royal policy, drawn from various treatises. This treatise is the seed of all, a compendium of the essence of Cāṇakya.

Mūlasuttaṃ pavakkhāmi, cāṇakyena yathoditaṃ;

Yassaṃ viññātamattena, mūḷho bhavati paṇḍito.

I will proclaim the root text, as it was taught by Cāṇakya, by the mere understanding of which a fool becomes a wise man.

Mittalābho suhadabhedo, viggaho sandhireva ca;

Pañcatandrā tathāññasmā, ganthā kassiyalikhyate.

The gaining of friends, the separation of friends, conflict, and alliance; material from the Pañcatantra and also from other books is written herein.

Lokanīti –

The Lokanīti –

Paṇḍito sujano kaṇḍo, dujjano mittaitthī ca;

Rājapakiṇṇako cāti, sattakaṇḍe vibhūsino.

The wise person, the good person, the wicked person, the friend, the woman, the king, and the miscellaneous section: thus it is adorned with seven chapters.

Cakkindābhisirināyaṃ, sodhito kāsike sāke;

Chanotyaṃ dutiyāsaḷhe, kāḷasattama ādihe.

Corrected by Cakkindābhisiri in the Kāsika era, at Chanot, on the second day of Āsāḷha, the seventh day of the dark fortnight.

Lokanītiṃ pavakkhāmi, nānāsatthasamuddhaṭaṃ;

Māgadheneva saṅkhepaṃ, vanditvā ratanattayaṃ.

Having paid homage to the Triple Gem, I shall proclaim the Lokanīti in brief in the Māgadhī tongue, a work compiled from various treatises.

Nīti loke purisassa sāro,

Mātā pitā ācariyo mitto;

Tasmā hi nītiṃ puriso vijaññā,

Ñāṇīmahā hoti bahussuto.

In this world, right conduct is the essence of a person. Mother, father, teacher, and friend—therefore a person should understand right conduct; he becomes greatly wise and very learned.

The Mahārahanīti –

(1) Paṇḍitakathā. (2) Sambhedakathā. (3) Mittakathā. (4) Nāyaka kathā. (5) Itthikathā

(1) The Discourse on the Wise; (2) The Discourse on Creating Division; (3) The Discourse on Friends; (4) The Discourse on Leaders; (5) The Discourse on Women.

Mahāraha rahaṃsakya-muniṃ nīvaraṇā taṇhā;

Muttaṃ muttaṃ sudassanaṃ, vande bodhivaraṃ varaṃ.

I pay homage to the Sakyan Sage, the great and worthy one, liberated from hindrances and craving, truly liberated, beautiful to see, the supreme, excellent Enlightened One.

Nītidha jantūnaṃ sāro, mittācariyā ca pitaro;

Nītimā subuddhibyatto, sutavā atthadassimā.

Here, wise conduct is the essence of beings, learned from friends, teachers, and parents. One who possesses such conduct is wise and articulate, learned and discerning of what is beneficial.

Cakkāticakkacakkindo, devātidevādevindo,

Brahmāti brahmabrahmindo, jino pūretu me bhāvaṃ.

Lord of Wheel-Turners, surpassing wheel-turners; Lord of Devas, a deva beyond devas; Lord of Brahmās, a brahmā beyond brahmās—may the Victor fulfill my aspiration.

Ciraṃ tiṭṭhatu lokamhi, dhaṃsakaṃ sabbapāṇinaṃ;

Mahāmohatamaṃ jayaṃ, jotantaṃ jinasāsanaṃ.

Long may the teaching of the Victor endure in the world, shining brightly, conquering the great darkness of delusion that is the destroyer of all living beings.

Vanditvā ratanaṃ seṭṭhaṃ, nissāya pubbake garu;

Nītidhammaṃ pavakkhāmi, sabbaloka sukhāvahaṃ.

Having paid homage to the supreme Jewel and relying on the teachers of old, I shall proclaim the Nītidhamma, which brings happiness to all the world.

Ācariyo ca sippañca, paññāsutakathādhanaṃ;

Desañca nissayo mittaṃ, dujjano sujano balaṃ.

The teacher and craft; wisdom, learning, discourse, and wealth; country, support, and friend; the wicked, the good, and strength.

Itthī putto ca dāso ca, gharāvāso katākato;

Ñātabbo ca alaṅkāro, rājadhammā pasevako;

Dukkhādimissako ceva, pakiṇṇakāti mātikā.

Woman, son, and servant; household life, what is to be done and not to be done; what is to be known and adornments; the principles of kingship and the attendant; mixed matters such as suffering; and miscellaneous—this is the table of contents.

Rājanīti –

The Rājanīti –

Sīhā ekaṃ bakā ekaṃ, sikkhe cattāri kukkuṭā;

Pañca kākā rājā nāma, cha sunakkhā tīṇi gadrabhā.

A king should learn one thing from a lion, one from a heron, four from a rooster, five from a crow, six from a dog, and three from a donkey.

1.

1.

Mahākammaṃ khuddakaṃ vā, yaṃ kammaṃ kātumicchati;

Sabbārambhena kātabbaṃ, sīhā ekaṃ tadā bhave.

Whether the task is great or small, whatever work one wishes to do, it should be done with total effort. This is the one quality to be learned from the lion.

2.

2.

Indriyāni susaṃyama, bakova paṇḍito bhave;

Desaka lomapannāni, sabbakammāni sādhaye.

Having well restrained his senses, the wise person should be like a heron; endowed with an understanding of place and time, he should accomplish all his tasks.

3.

3.

Pubbaṭṭhānañca yuddhañca, saṃvibhāgañca bandhu hi;

Thiyā akkamma bhuttañca, sikkhe cattāri kukkuṭo.

Rising early, fighting, sharing with relatives, and forcefully enjoying a female—these four things are to be learned from a rooster.

4.

4.

Guyhe methunaṃ pekkhitvā, bhojanaṃ ñātisaṅgaho;

Vilokā pekkhanālasyaṃ, pañca sikkheyya vāyasā.

Secret intercourse, eating with vigilance, gathering with kin, watchfulness, and diligence—these five should one learn from a crow.

5.

5.

Anālassaṃtisavantāso, suniddhā suppabodhanā;

Daḷhabhatti ca sūrañca, cha etesvānato guṇo.

Diligence, great contentment, sleeping soundly, waking easily, firm loyalty, and bravery—these six are the qualities to be learned from a dog.

6.

6.

Khinnova vahate bhāraṃ, sītuṇhañca na cintayī;

Santuṭṭho ca bhave niccaṃ, tīṇi sikkheyya gadrabhā.

To carry one's burden even when exhausted, to be indifferent to cold and heat, and to be always content—these three things should be learned from a donkey.

7.

7.

Vīsati tāni guṇāni, careyya iha paṇḍito;

Vijeyya ripū sabbepi, tejassī so bhavissati.

The wise person who practices these twenty qualities in this world will conquer all his enemies and become full of majesty.

(1) Paṇḍitakaṇḍa (2) sujanakaṇḍa (3) bāladujjanakaṇḍa (4) mittakaṇḍa (5) rājakaṇḍa (6) nāyakakaṇḍa (7) puttakaṇḍa (8) vejjācariyakaṇḍa (9) dāsakakaṇḍa (10) itthikaṇḍa (11) pakiṇṇakakaṇḍa

(1) The Section on the Wise; (2) The Section on Good People; (3) The Section on Fools and the Wicked; (4) The Section on Friends; (5) The Section on Kings; (6) The Section on Leaders; (7) The Section on Sons; (8) The Section on Physicians and Teachers; (9) The Section on Servants; (10) The Section on Women; (11) The Miscellaneous Section.

Kavidappaṇanītiṃyo, vācuggataṃ karoti ce;

Bhuvanamajjhe eso hi, viññū paṇḍitajātiko.

Whoever learns the Kavidappaṇanīti by heart, that person, in the midst of the world, is truly intelligent, one of the wise.

Kavidappaṇanīti

The Poet's Mirror

Namo tassa bhagavato arahato sammāsambuddhassa

Homage to the Blessed One, the Arahant, the Perfectly Self-Enlightened One.

Ratanattayapaṇāma

Homage to the Triple Gem

1.

1.

Mahākavivaraṃ [M 0.0001] buddhaṃ, dhammañca tena sevitaṃ;

Saṅghaṃ niraṅgaṇañcāpi, vandāmi sirasā daraṃ.

With reverence, I bow my head to the Buddha, the great and excellent sage; to the Dhamma cultivated by him; and also to the stainless Sangha.

2.

2.

Karissāmi samāsena, nānāsattha samuddhaṭaṃ;

Hitāya kavinaṃ nītiṃ, kavidappaṇanāmakaṃ.

I will compose concisely, having extracted from various scriptures, a guide for the benefit of poets, named 'The Poet's Mirror'.

Paṇḍitakaṇḍa

The Section on the Wise

3.

3.

Nīti [M 0.0002] sāro manussānaṃ, mitto ācariyopi ca;

Mātā pitā ca nītimā, sutavā ganthakārako.

Guidance is the essence for human beings; it is a friend and a teacher, a wise mother and father, a learned person, and an author.

4.

4.

Alasassa [M 0.0003] kuto sippaṃ, asippassa kuto dhanaṃ;

Adhanassa kuto mittaṃ, amittassa kuto sukhaṃ;

Asukhassa kuto puññaṃ, apuññassa kuto varaṃ.

For the lazy, whence is skill? For the unskilled, whence is wealth? For the poor, whence is a friend? For the friendless, whence is happiness? For the unhappy, whence is merit? For the meritless, whence is a boon?

5.

5.

Sucintitacintī [M 0.0004] ceva, subhāsitabhāsīpi ca;

Sukatakammakārī ca, paṇḍito sādhumānuso.

One who thinks well-thought thoughts, speaks well-spoken words, and does well-done deeds is a wise and good person.

6.

6.

Kaviheraññakā [M 0.0005] katvā, uttattaṃ satthakañcanaṃ;

Bhūsanaṃ gajjapajjādiṃ, karonti ca manoharaṃ.

Poets, who are like goldsmiths, having refined the gold of the scriptures, create charming ornaments such as prose and verse.

7.

7.

Bahuṃ [M 0.0006] lahuñca gahaṇaṃ, sammūpadhāraṇampi ca;

Gahita asammussanaṃ, etaṃ suviññulakkhaṇaṃ.

Grasping much and quickly, proper retention, and not forgetting what has been grasped—this is the mark of a truly wise person.

8.

8.

Ajarāmaraṃva pañño, vijjamatthañca cintaye;

Gahito iva kesesu, maccunā dhammamācare.

The wise person should pursue knowledge and wealth as if not subject to old age and death; but as if seized by the hair by Death, they should practice the Dhamma.

9.

9.

Sippasamaṃ [M 0.0007] dhanaṃ natthi, sippaṃ corā na gaṇhare;

Idha loke sippaṃ mittaṃ, paraloke sukhāvahaṃ.

There is no wealth equal to skill; thieves cannot take skill. In this world skill is a friend; in the next world it brings happiness.

10.

10.

Bhuñjanatthaṃ [M 0.0008] kathanatthaṃ, mukhaṃ hotīti no vade;

Yaṃ vātaṃ vā mukhāruḷhaṃ, vacanaṃ paṇḍito naro.

A wise person should not utter whatever random word comes to their mouth, thinking, 'The mouth is for eating and speaking.'

11.

11.

Dummedhehi pasaṃsā ca, viññūhi garahā ca yā;

Garahāva seyyo viññūhi, yañce bālappasaṃsanā.

Praise from the foolish and blame from the wise—blame from the wise is surely better than praise from fools.

12.

12.

Acintiye [M 0.0009] sāṭṭhakathe, paṇḍito jinabhāsite;

Upadesaṃ sadā gaṇhe, garuṃ sammā upaṭṭhahaṃ.

In the profound, commented-upon speech of the Conqueror, the wise person should always receive instruction, properly attending to the teacher.

Tasmā sāṭṭhakathe dhīro, gambhīre jinabhāsite;

Upadesaṃ sadā gaṇhe, garuṃ sammā upaṭṭhahaṃ.

Therefore, in the profound, commented-upon speech of the Conqueror, the steadfast person should always receive instruction, properly attending to the teacher.

13.

13.

Garūpadesahīno [M 0.0010] hi, atthasāraṃ na vindati;

Atthasāravihīno so, saddhammā parihāyati.

Indeed, one who lacks a teacher's instruction does not find the essence of the meaning; deprived of the essence of the meaning, one falls away from the true Dhamma.

14.

14.

Garūpadesalābhī ca, atthasārasamāyuto;

Saddhammaṃ paripālento, saddhammasmā na hāyati.

One who obtains a teacher's instruction, is endowed with the essence of the meaning, and protects the true Dhamma, does not fall away from the true Dhamma.

15.

15.

Sabbadabbesu [M 0.0011] vijjeva, dabbamāhu anuttaraṃ;

Ahārattā anagghattā, akkhayattā ca sabbadā.

Among all possessions, they call knowledge the unsurpassed possession, because it cannot be stolen, is priceless, and is always inexhaustible.

16.

16.

Appakenapi medhāvī, pābhatena vicakkhaṇo;

Samuṭṭhāpeti attānaṃ, aṇuṃ aggiṃva sandhamaṃ.

Even with little, the wise and discerning person raises themself up, like one fanning a tiny fire.

17.

17.

Paṇḍite [M 0.0012] ca guṇā sabbe, mūḷhe dosā hi kevalaṃ;

Tasmā mūḷhasahassesu, pañño eko visesiyate.

In the wise are all virtues; in the fool, only faults. Therefore, one wise person is more distinguished than thousands of fools.

18.

18.

Bālā [M 0.0013] issanti dummedhā, guṇī niddosakārino;

Garuko paṇḍito etasa-missaṃ tehyavidvā samo.

Fools and the unwise are envious; the virtuous do what is blameless. The wise person is venerable; no ignorant one is their equal.

19.

19.

Manuññameva [M 0.0014] bhāseyya, nāmanuññaṃ kudācanaṃ;

Manuññaṃ bhāsamānassa, garuṃ bhāraṃ udaddhari;

Dhanañca naṃ alābhesi, tena cattamano ahu.

One should speak only what is pleasing, never what is unpleasing. By speaking what was pleasing, he lifted a heavy burden, obtained wealth, and because of that, his mind was delighted.

20.

20.

Vijjā [M 0.0015] dadāti vinayaṃ, vinayā yāti pattataṃ;

Pattattā dhanaṃ pappoti, dhanā dhammaṃ tato sukhaṃ.

Knowledge gives discipline; from discipline one attains fitness. From fitness one obtains wealth; from wealth, Dhamma; and from that, happiness.

21.

21.

Ye vuḍḍhamapacayanti, narā dhammassa kovidā;

Diṭṭheva dhamme pāsaṃsā, samparāye ca suggatiṃ.

Those people, wise in the Dhamma, who honor their elders, are praiseworthy in this very life and find a good destination in the hereafter.

22.

22.

Mātariva [M 0.0016] paradāresu, paradabbesu ledduṃva;

Attanīva sabbabhūtesu, yo passati so paṇḍito.

He who looks upon another's wife as his mother, upon another's property as a clod of earth, and upon all beings as himself—he is a wise person.

23.

23.

Āsīsetheva [M 0.0017] puriso, na nibbindeyya paṇḍito;

Anavajjesu kammesu, pasaṃsitesu sādhubhi.

A person should always aspire, the wise should not become disheartened, in regard to blameless actions praised by the good.

Āsīsetheva [M 0.0018] puriso, na nibbindeyya paṇḍito;

Passāmi vohaṃ attānaṃ, yathā icchiṃ tathā ahu.

A person should always aspire, the wise should not become disheartened. I see myself: just as I wished, so it has come to be.

24.

24.

Vāyametheva puriso, na nibbindeyya paṇḍito;

Puññakriyavatthūsu, pasaṃsitesu viññubhi.

A person should always strive, the wise should not become disheartened, in the grounds for meritorious action praised by the discerning.

25.

25.

Loke [M 0.0019] ussāhavantānaṃ, janānaṃ kimasādhiyaṃ;

Sāgarepi mahāsetuṃ, kapiyūthehi bandhati.

For energetic people in this world, what is unachievable? A great bridge was built even across the ocean by troops of monkeys.

26.

26.

Kiṃ kulena visālena, guṇahīno tu yo naro;

Akulinopi satthañño, devatāhipi pujjate.

What is a great lineage to a person who is devoid of virtue? Even one of no lineage, if wise in the teachings, is honored even by the gods.

27.

27.

Ukkaṭṭhe [M 0.0020] sūramicchanti, mantīsu akutūhalaṃ;

Piyañca annapānamhi, atthe jāte ca paṇḍitaṃ.

In a crisis, they desire a hero; in counsel, one who is steadfast; at a meal, a dear one; and when a need arises, a wise person.

28.

28.

Rūpayobbannasampannā[M 0.0021], visālakulasambhavā;

Vijjāhīnā na sobhante, niggandhā iva kiṃ sukā.

Though endowed with beauty and youth and born of a great family, those lacking knowledge do not shine, like scentless kiṃsuka flowers.

29.

29.

Vutyaṃ [M 0.0022] visadañāṇassa, ñāto attho tarassana;

Sūrappabhāya ādāso, chāyaṃ disse na mākare.

For one with clear knowledge, the meaning is understood as a way to cross over; just as in a mirror in the sun's light, a reflection is seen, not made.

30.

30.

Aveyyākaraṇo tvandho, badhiro kosavajjito;

Sāhiccarahito paṅgu, mūgo takkavivajjito.

One without grammar is blind; one without a treasury of knowledge is deaf; one without literature is lame; one without logic is mute.

31.

31.

Dhīro [M 0.0023] ca vividhānaññū, paresaṃ vivarānugū;

Sabbāmitte vasīkatvā, kosiyova sukhī siyā.

The wise one, a knower of various things, who is compliant with the ways of others, having brought all enemies under control, may be happy like Kosiya.

32.

32.

Mahātejopi [M 0.0024] tejoyaṃ, mattikaṃ na muduṃ kare;

Āpo āpesi mudukaṃ, sādhuvācāva kakkhaḷaṃ.

Even a great fire does not soften clay, but water makes it soft; just so, gentle speech softens a harsh person.

33.

33.

Kottho puttena jātena, yo na vidū na dhammiko;

Kāṇena cakkhunā kiṃ vā, cakkhu pīḷeva kevalaṃ.

What is the use of a son who is born, if he is neither wise nor righteous? Or what is the use of a defective eye? It is merely a source of affliction.

34.

34.

Mudunāva [M 0.0025] ripuṃ jeti, mudunā jeti dāruṇaṃ;

No na siddhaṃ mudu kiñci, tato ca mudunā jaye.

With gentleness one conquers an enemy; with gentleness one conquers the cruel. There is nothing that cannot be accomplished with gentleness; therefore, one should conquer with gentleness.

35.

35.

Sajāto yena jātena, yāti vaṃso samunnatiṃ;

Parivattinisaṃsāre, mato ko vā na jāyate.

He is truly born by whose birth the family line is exalted. For in this revolving saṃsāra, who that has died is not born again?

36.

36.

Dāne [M 0.0026] tapasi sūre ca, yassa na patthito yaso;

Vijjāya matthalābhe ca, kevalaṃ adhikovaso.

One for whom fame is not sought in giving, austerity, or valor, nor in knowledge and the attainment of a goal, is merely a master of themself.

37.

37.

Varo eko guṇī putto, na ca mūḷhasatānyapi;

Eko cando tamo hanati, na ca tārāgaṇo tathā.

One virtuous son is better than even hundreds of foolish ones; one moon dispels the darkness, but a host of stars does not do so.

38.

38.

Puññatitthakato yena, tapo kvāpi sudukkaro;

Tassa putto bhave vasso, samiddho dhammiko suddhe.

He by whom a ford of merit has been made and a most difficult austerity practiced somewhere, his son will be obedient, prosperous, righteous, and pure.

39.

39.

Lālaye pañcavassāni, dasavassāni tālaye;

Pattetu soḷase vasse, puttaṃ mittaṃva ācare.

Sandalwood is cool in the world; cooler than that is moonlight. Yet a good, well-spoken word is cooler even than sandalwood and moonlight.

A short word spoken at the proper time is a well-spoken utterance, just as even poor food, when eaten by a hungry person, becomes delicious.

Even teachers may be garrulous, and those who speak much are not respected. The helpful are few and the indifferent are many—is this not seen, like the water of a river?

Among hundreds a hero is born; among thousands, a wise person; among a hundred thousand, a speaker; but a giver may or may not be found.

Scholarship and kingship are never equal. A king is honored in his own country, but a scholar is honored everywhere.

If oneself is well-disciplined, the great populace will rely on one. All will follow the disciplined one; what wise person would destroy him?

Between the body and virtues, the difference is exceedingly vast. The body is destroyed in a moment; virtues last to the end of the aeon.

Truth, and again truth!—it is declared with arm upraised. There is no benefit for oneself whatsoever for one who does not act for the benefit of another.

The mind of the virtuous is not agitated by harsh words, just as it is not possible to heat the water of the ocean with grass torches.

Just as a solid rock is not shaken by the wind, so the wise are not moved by blame and praise.

For one in whom not even one of these—Dhamma, wealth, pleasure, or liberation—is found, their birth is useless, like the fleshy appendage on a goat's neck.

One should not abandon one's own exertion by thinking only of kamma; without effort, oil cannot be obtained from sesame seeds.

Just as with a single wheel there is no movement of a chariot, so too, without human effort, destiny does not succeed.

Tasks are accomplished by effort, not by wishes; indeed, deer do not enter the mouth of a sleeping lion.

A child trained by mother and father attains virtue; a son does not become wise by mere birth from the womb.

The mother is an enemy and the father a foe by whom a child is not educated. Such a one does not shine in the midst of an assembly, like a heron in the midst of swans.

Glass, through association with gold, takes on the radiance of an emerald; so too, through the company of the virtuous, a fool attains proficiency.

Therefore, one who desires his own welfare should rightly acquire skill in letters, properly attending upon the teacher with the five duties beginning with rising.

Sakkaccaṃ sippuggahaṇā, garuṃ ārādhaye budho.

By rising, by attending, by obedience, by personal service, and by respectfully grasping the skill, a wise person should win over the teacher.

Byasanena ca mūḷhānaṃ, nidāya kalahena vā.

The time of the wise passes in the enjoyment of poetry and treatises; but for the foolish, it passes in vice, sleep, or quarreling.

Six faults should be abandoned here by a person who desires prosperity: sleepiness, lethargy, fear, anger, idleness, and procrastination.

The person who is fond of sleep, fond of company, unenergetic, lazy, and marked by anger—that is a cause of downfall.

The virtuous show compassion even toward beings devoid of good qualities; for the moon does not withhold its light from the dwelling of an outcaste.

Teeth, hair, nails, and men do not look well when they are out of place; having understood this, a wise person should not abandon their own station.

Dhamme sayamanuṭṭhānaṃ, kassacisumahattano.

Cleverness in advising others is indeed easy for everyone; but to practice the Dhamma oneself is the mark of some truly great person.

The wise praise diligence in performing meritorious deeds; the diligent wise person secures both benefits.

One should regard a wise person who points out one’s faults and speaks reprovingly as a revealer of hidden treasure. One should associate with such a sage. For one who associates with such a person, it is better, not worse.

If an intelligent person associates with a wise person even for a moment, he quickly understands the Dhamma, just as the tongue perceives the taste of soup.

Yattha so jāyatī dhīro, taṃ kulaṃ sukha medhati.

A thoroughbred of a man is rare; he is not born everywhere. Wherever that wise one is born, that family prospers happily.

Pattāpi surati vāyanti, evaṃ dhīrūpasevanā.

If a person wraps tagara powder in a palāsa leaf, the leaves themselves will emit a fragrance; such is association with the wise.

One who seeks the meaning of what is heard should skillfully investigate it; just as rice cooked in a pot should then be in the bowl, so should be what is heard.

One should not disdain even a little of what is heard, but should deposit it in the mind; like an anthill, by gradual accumulation it becomes full in due course.

The ant, by going, travels even hundreds of leagues; but Vainateya, by not going, does not move a single step.

If one seeking learning should hear, 'There is a wise person endowed with learning,' one should go to that place with great effort.

A skill that is only in books and wealth that is in the hands of others: when a need arises, that is not a real skill, nor is that real wealth.

In the first stage of life one should acquire a skill; in the second, one should seek wealth; in the third, one should practice the Dhamma. This is the natural way for people.

The desire to listen increases learning; learning increases wisdom. Through wisdom one knows the meaning, and the meaning, once known, brings happiness.

A hero should not go to the battlefield without a weapon; nor should a wise man, a traveler, or a merchant go to a foreign land unprepared.

Vañcanañca avamānaṃ, paṇḍito na pakāsaye.

A wise person should not disclose the loss of wealth, mental anguish, misconduct in his own home, or that he has been deceived or humiliated.

One who goes uninvited, one who speaks much without being asked, and one who proclaims his own good qualities: these are the three kinds of inferior persons.

A swan does not shine among crows, a lion does not look well among cattle; nor a steed among donkeys, nor a wise person among fools.

Attānurūpakaṃ kodhaṃ, yo jānāti sa paṇḍito.

He who knows speech appropriate to the listener, affection appropriate to his own nature, and anger appropriate to his own capacity—he is a wise person.

An insignificant person talks a lot; a person of little wisdom boasts. A half-full pot makes much noise; a cow with little milk is restless.

A king is not satisfied with wealth, nor a wise person with well-spoken words; the eye is not satisfied with seeing what is dear, nor the ocean with water.

A king's minister may be the son of a humble man; a wise man may be the son of a fool; much wealth may come to one who was poor. Therefore, one should not look down on men.

Mūgova supinaṃ passaṃ, kathetumpi na ussahe.

The student who, out of greed for skill, acquires that skill in excess, is like a mute person who has seen a dream but is unable even to describe it.

A potter strives to beautify a pot, not to break it. Similarly, a teacher disciplines his students for the sake of their growth, not to cast them into states of woe.

100.

Appaññassa vākyakaro, ummattaka samāhikho.

For a poor person, a taste for delicacies is a torment; for a weak person, a powerful man is a threat. One who follows the words of a fool is indeed like a madman.

101.

Vāsitaṃ kānanaṃ sabbaṃ, suputtena kulaṃ yathā.

By a single fine tree, flowering and fragrant, the entire forest is perfumed; just so is a family by a good son.

Bhariyā rūpavatī sattu, putto sattu apaṇḍito.

A father in debt is an enemy, and an unchaste mother is an enemy; a beautiful wife is an enemy, and an unwise son is an enemy.

Adhikāro kimandhassa, rūpabhedopaladdhiyaṃ.

How can a person who does not know the treatises on virtues and faults distinguish between them? What ability has a blind person to perceive the differences between forms?

Yaṃ karoti vināsatthaṃ, sāhasaṃ kimatodhikaṃ.

In all matters, the discernment of virtues and faults is from the treatises. What could be more reckless than to act without the treatises?

105.

Seṭṭhamupanamaṃ udeti khippaṃ,

Tasmā attano uttariṃ bhaje.

A person who associates with the inferior declines; he would never decline by associating with an equal. By approaching the superior one rises up quickly; therefore one should associate with those superior to oneself.

Asaṃhīraṃ asaṃkuppaṃ, taṃ vidvā manubrūhaye.

Whoever has insight into a present state, right where it is, should cultivate that state, knowing it to be unassailable and unshakable.

Āpūrati tassa yaso, sukkapakkheva candimā.

Whoever does not transgress the Dhamma out of desire, hatred, fear, or delusion, his fame increases like the moon in the bright fortnight.

108.

Paṇḍito [M 0.0067] sīlasampanno, saṇho ca paṭibhānavā;

Nivātavutti athaddho, tādiso labhate yasaṃ.

The wise person who is endowed with virtue, gentle and eloquent, humble in manner and not stubborn—such a one obtains fame.

Dullabhā sadisī jāyā, dullabho sajano piyo.

Rare is a natural utterance, rare is a son who brings security; rare is a wife who is of a similar mind, rare is a dear kinsman.

110.

Careyyāmānathaddho yo, paṇḍito so pavuccati.

He who attains a great goal, knowledge, and success, yet conducts himself without conceit or stubbornness—he is called a wise person.

Puṇṇāsubhāsitenāpi, ghaṇṭādī ghaṭṭitā yathā.

The wise, having accumulated learning, are silent when unasked; though full of well-spoken words, they are like a bell that sounds only when struck.

When unasked, a wise person is like a drum; when asked, he thunders like a rain cloud. But a fool, whether asked or unasked, always boasts a great deal.

113.

Paṅgulo aññanārīsu, dussatakke acetano.

He is deaf to the criticism of others, blind to others' faults; lame in regard to other women, and senseless concerning wicked plots.

Paññavāssa yathāmūgo, balavā dubbaloriva;

Atha atthe samuppanne, sayetha matasāyitaṃ.

Though having eyes, one should be as if blind; though having ears, as if deaf; though having wisdom, as if mute; though strong, as if a weakling. Then, when a matter arises, one should lie as if dead.

Ovāde cassa tiṭṭheyya, patthento acalaṃ sukhaṃ.

Having avoided bad friends, one should associate with the best of persons. Aspiring to unshakable happiness, one should stand by their advice.

Iti vissaṭṭhakammante, atthā accenti māṇave.

'It is too cold, it is too hot, it is too late in the evening'—thus men neglect their work, and opportunities pass them by.

But he who regards cold and heat as no more than a blade of grass, and performs his manly duties, does not fall away from happiness.

Na ca vijjāgamo koci, na tattha divasaṃ vase.

In a country where there is no respect, no affection, and no kinsmen, and where there is no acquisition of knowledge, one should not stay there even for a day.

Yattha dese na vijjanti, na tattha divasaṃ vase.

A rich man, a learned man, a king, a river, and a physician: where these five are not to be found, one should not live in that country for even a day.

Chamāya bhūsanaṃ rājā, vijjā sabbassa bhūsanaṃ.

The moon is the ornament of the sky; a husband is the ornament of a woman. The king is the ornament of the earth; knowledge is the ornament of all.

If one seeks comfort, one should give up learning; if one seeks learning, one should give up comfort. For one who seeks comfort, how can there be learning? For one who seeks learning, how can there be comfort?

One should acquire knowledge and wealth moment by moment, bit by bit. If one wastes moments, how can there be knowledge? If one wastes bits, how can there be wealth?

One learns a quarter from the teacher, a quarter by one's own intelligence, a quarter from one's fellow students, and a quarter in the course of time.

Dhamma conquers, not non-Dhamma; truth conquers, not falsehood. Forbearance conquers, not anger; the devas conquer, not the asuras.

Sotassa bhūsanaṃ satthaṃ, bhūsane kiṃ payojanaṃ.

Giving is the ornament of the hand; truth is the ornament of the throat. Learning is the ornament of the ear; what is the use of other ornaments?

Paraloke dhanaṃ dhammo, sīlaṃ sabbattha ve dhanaṃ.

In foreign lands, knowledge is wealth; in times of adversity, intelligence is wealth. In the next world, Dhamma is wealth; but virtue is wealth everywhere.

At night the moon is the lamp; in the daytime the sun is the lamp. In the three worlds the Dhamma is the lamp; a good son is the lamp of the family.

Only a learned person knows the toil of another learned person; a barren woman does not know the severe pain of childbirth.

For one who has no wisdom of his own, what can a scripture do? For a person who is deprived of his eyes, what will a mirror do?

What can speakers do where no listener is to be found? What can a dyer do in a country of naked ascetics?

Khalasattūhi saṃyogā, paṇḍitopyāvasīdati.

Even a wise man may come to ruin through a false accusation of folly, by supporting a bad wife, and from association with wicked enemies.

There is no love equal to love for oneself, no wealth equal to grain, no light equal to wisdom; rain is the best of liquids.

Bhikkhuñca sīlasampannaṃ, sammadeva samācare.

One should always treat rightly a snake, fire, a glorious noble, and a bhikkhu accomplished in virtue.

Buddhe dhamme ca saṅghe ca, dhīro saddhaṃ nivesaye.

Therefore a wise person, seeing his own welfare, should establish faith in the Buddha, the Dhamma, and the Sangha.

Pāsādasikhare vāso, kāko kiṃ garuḷo siyā.

Virtue attains the highest state, not by dwelling on a lofty couch. By living on a palace peak, how could a crow become a garuḍa?

Āgatañca bhayaṃ disvā, abhīto hoti paṇḍito.

Seeing a future danger, one should avoid it from afar; seeing a danger that has come, the wise person remains fearless.

Malaṃ vaṇṇassa kosajjaṃ, pamādo rakkhato malaṃ.

Non-recitation is the stain of sacred texts, non-exertion is the stain of households, laziness is the stain of beauty, and heedlessness is the stain of a guardian.

Kammāro rajatasseva, niddhame malamattano.

Gradually, little by little, moment by moment, the wise person should remove his own stain, as a smith removes the dross from silver.

Akarontaṃ bhāsamānaṃ, parijānanti paṇḍitā.

What one does, that one should say; what one does not do, that one should not say. The wise recognize the one who speaks but does not do.

Uneven ground, a place of fear, excessive wind, a concealed place, a place inhabited by a deity, a path, a battle, and a ford—these eight should be avoided.

Ekacintī ca bālo ca, ete atthavināsakā.

The lustful, the hateful, and the deluded; the conceited, the greedy, and the lazy; the stubborn and the fool—these are destroyers of one's welfare.

Itthī soṇḍo paṇḍako ca, navamo dārakopi ca.

The lustful, the malicious, and the deluded; the fearful and the one greedy for gain; a woman, a drunkard, and a eunuch; and the ninth is a child.

Etehi mantitaṃ guyhaṃ, khippaṃ bhavati pākaṭaṃ.

These nine persons in the world are fickle, agitated, and unsteady. A secret confided to them quickly becomes public.

Yo [M 0.0086] niruttiṃ na sikkheyya, sikkhanto piṭakattayaṃ;

Pade pade vikaṅkheyya, vane andhagajo yathā.

One who does not learn grammar while studying the Tipiṭaka would be doubtful at every step, like a blind elephant in the forest.

Suttaṃ [M 0.0087] dhātu gaṇoṇvādi, nāmaliṅgānusāsanaṃ;

Yassa tiṭṭhati jivhagge, sabyākaraṇakesarī.

The grammatical aphorisms, the verbal roots, the verb classes and uṇādi suffixes, the rules of nouns and gender—he on the tip of whose tongue these rest is a lion of grammar.

So so ñātumakicchena, pahoti piṭakattaye.

Whoever discerns the characteristics of words and their meanings, and has ascertained their features, can with little difficulty become proficient in the Tipiṭaka.

Chando alaṅkatisu niccakatābhiyogo;

So yaṃ kavittavikalopi kavīsu saṅkhyaṃ,

Moggayha vindati hi kitti’ mamandarūpaṃ.

He who is skilled in the science of words and expert in lexicons, constantly engaged in prosody and figures of speech—even if deficient in poetic ability, he is counted among the poets and attains widespread fame.

Nāgo gato naramukhe na jahāti līḷaṃ;

Yantagato madhurasaṃ na jahāti ucchu,

Dukkhopi paṇḍitajano na jahāti dhammaṃ.

The sandalwood tree, though dry, does not give up its fragrance; the elephant, though facing death, does not abandon its graceful bearing; the sugarcane, though put in a press, does not give up its sweetness; the wise person, though in distress, does not abandon the Dhamma.

Dhanadhaññappayogesu, tathā vijjāgamesu ca;

Āhāre byavahāre ca, cattalajjo sadā bhave.

In the use of wealth and grain, and likewise in the acquisition of knowledge, and in matters of food and business, one should always be without shame.

Sābhāvikī [M 0.0090] ca paṭibhā, sutañca bahunimmalaṃ;

Amando cābhiyogoyaṃ, hetu hotiha bandhane.

Natural intelligence and wit, much immaculate learning, and diligent application—this is the cause here for poetic composition.

Sādhavo abhiseveyya, suṇeyya dhammamuttamaṃ.

One should abandon evil friends and associate with wise people; one should attend upon the virtuous and listen to the sublime Dhamma.

Yādisaṃ vappate bījaṃ, tādisaṃ harate phalaṃ.

The doer of good reaps good, and the doer of evil reaps evil. Just as is the seed that is sown, so is the fruit that one reaps.

152.

Chando [M 0.0092] nidānaṃ gāthānaṃ, akkharā tāsaṃ viyañjanaṃ;

Nāmasannissitā gāthā, kavi gāthānamāsayo.

Meter is the source of verses, letters are their expression; verses are dependent on words, the poet is the repository of verses.

153.

Tasmā [M 0.0093] hi paṇḍito poso, sampassaṃ hitamattano;

Paññavantaṃbhipūjeyya, cetiyaṃ viya sādaro.

Therefore a wise person, seeing his own welfare, should respectfully revere one endowed with wisdom, as one would a shrine.

Dhīrenallāpasallāpaṃ, taṃ kare tañca rocaye.

One should see the wise, listen to the wise, and live together with the wise. One should engage in friendly conversation with the wise; one should do as they do and approve of it.

Sunayo seyyaso hoti, sammā vutto na kuppati;

Vinayaṃ so pajānāti, sādhu tena samāgamo.

The wise person leads with prudence and does not engage with the unruly. A person of good conduct is superior; when spoken to rightly, he does not become angry. He understands discipline; association with such a one is good.

156.

Saddhiṃ caraṃ sādhuvihāri dhīraṃ;

Abhibhuyya sabbāni parissayāni,

Careyya tenattamano satimā.

If one should find a prudent companion, a steadfast and virtuous fellow traveler, one should walk with him, gratified and mindful, having overcome all dangers.

Saddhiṃ caraṃ sādhuvihāri dhīraṃ;

Rājāva raṭṭhaṃ vijitaṃ pahāya,

Eko care mātaṅgaraññeva nāgo.

If one should not find a prudent companion, a steadfast and virtuous fellow traveler, then like a king who has abandoned his conquered kingdom, one should wander alone like an elephant in the elephant forest.

Divase divase mūḷha-māvisanti na paṇḍitaṃ.

Thousands of occasions for sorrow and hundreds of occasions for fear assail the fool day by day, but not the wise person.

Tathā sakalavijjānaṃ, dhammassa ca dhanassa ca.

By the falling of water drops, a pot is gradually filled; so it is with the accumulation of all knowledge, of the Dhamma, and of wealth.

Pavissa rāhuno mukhaṃ, kiṃ no deti puna sasī.

The wise, having encountered suffering, do not become despondent. Having entered the mouth of Rāhu, does the moon not emerge again?

161.

Duhena dhenuṃ jānanti, bhāsamānena paṇḍitaṃ.

One knows a horse by its speed, a draught ox by what it carries, a milk-cow by its milking, and a wise person by their speech.

Aññalakkhitakāriyassa, yato siddhi na jāyate.

A deed conceived in the mind one should not reveal by speech, for when one's task is perceived by another, success is not achieved.

Visaṃ sabhā daliddassa, vuddhassa taruṇī visaṃ.

Knowledge without practice is poison; food during indigestion is poison; an assembly is poison for the poor; a young woman is poison for an old man.

Alaṃ phāsuvihārāya, pahitattassa bhikkhuno.

Having eaten four or five morsels, one should drink water; this is sufficient for the comfortable abiding of a resolute bhikkhu.

A virtuous man is honored even if of low birth; what can a virtueless man accomplish, even if of pure lineage?

Parabhāgyopajīvī ca, chaḷete dukkhabhāgino.

The envious, the ungenerous, the discontented, the angry, the ever-suspicious, and one who lives off the fortune of another—these six are partakers in suffering.

Chettāro saṃsayānañca, kaliṃ yanti lobhamohitā.

Even very great beings, the very learned who uphold the teachings and are cutters of doubts, go to strife when they are bewildered by greed.

Na vade dakādī natthīti, mukhe ca vacanaṃ tathā.

Concerning a riverbank, a dug well, a fire-stick, or a palm stalk, one should not say, 'There is no water'; likewise, one should not be hasty with a word from the mouth.

Na ca diṭṭhaṃ sutaṃ dhīro, sabbaṃ uccitu marahati.

One hears everything with the ear and sees everything with the eye, but a wise person ought not to proclaim everything seen and heard.

Ravissāvisaye kiṃ na, padīpassa pakāsanaṃ.

A reasonable saying should be accepted by the wise even from a fool. In the domain of the sun, is not the light of a lamp also illuminating?

Yoniso vicine dhammaṃ, evaṃ tattha visujjhati.

Therefore a wise person, seeing their own welfare, should wisely investigate the Dhamma; in this way they become purified.

What is the use of one born from the womb, a taker of his mother's youth, who does not rise like a banner at the head of his own lineage?

Coraghāto siyā sisso, guru coraṭṭakārako.

Having rightly investigated the letters and passages, the student might become a slayer of thieves, the teacher one who brings a case against a thief.

A teaching is for the taming of the untamed, but for the wicked it produces pride, just as light, which assists the eye, is blinding for owls.

Human birth is rare in the world; rarer still is knowledge. Poetic skill is rare therein; rarer still is the creative power.

For most beings, when destruction is imminent, wrong policy in the guise of right policy approaches and seizes the intellect.

Sujanakaṇḍa

The Section on the Good Person

176.

176.

Saddhāsīlādidhammehi, [M 0.0106] sappanno seṭṭhamānuso;

Vutto buddhādisantehi, sādhusappuriso iti.

Endowed with qualities like faith and virtue, the accomplished person is the best of humans; by the Buddhas and other peaceful ones he is declared to be a good and true person.

Saddādhanaṃ sīladhanaṃ, hirīottappiyaṃ dhanaṃ;

Sutadhanañca cāgo ca, paññā ve sattamaṃ dhanaṃ.

The wealth of faith, the wealth of virtue, the wealth of conscience and fear of wrongdoing, the wealth of learning, and generosity; wisdom is indeed the seventh wealth.

Yassa ete dhanā atthi, itthiyā purisassa vā;

Adaliddoti taṃ āhu, amoghaṃ tassa jīvitaṃ.

The woman or the man who possesses these treasures is called one who is not poor; their life is not lived in vain.

Tasmā saddhañca sīlañca, pasādaṃ dhammadassanaṃ;

Anuyuñjetha medhāvī, saraṃ buddhānasāsanaṃ.

Therefore, the wise person, remembering the teaching of the Buddhas, should be devoted to faith, virtue, confidence, and vision of the Dhamma.

177.

177.

Sabbhireva [M 0.0107] samāsetha, sabbhi kubbetha santhavaṃ;

Sataṃ saddhammamaññāya, seyyo hoti na pāpiyo.

One should associate only with the good; with the good one should cultivate intimacy. By understanding the True Dhamma of the good, one becomes better, not worse.

178.

178.

Caja dujjanasaṃsaggaṃ, bhaja sādhusamāgamaṃ;

Kara puññamahorattaṃ, sara niccamaniccataṃ.

Abandon association with the wicked, cultivate the company of the good; do meritorious deeds day and night, and constantly remember impermanence.

179.

179.

Yo [M 0.0108] ve kataññū katavedī dhīro,

Kalyāṇamitto daḷhabhatti ca hoti;

Dukkhitassa sakkacca karoti kiccaṃ,

Tathāvidhaṃ sappurisaṃ vadanti.

One who is grateful and acknowledges help received, who is wise, has good friends, and is firm in devotion; who diligently performs his task for one in distress—such a one they call a true person.

180.

180.

Mātāpettibharaṃ [M 0.0109] jantuṃ, kule jeṭṭhāpacāyinaṃ;

Saṇhaṃ sakhilasambhāsaṃ, pesuṇeyyappahāyinaṃ.

One who supports his mother and father, who honors the elders in his family, who is gentle and courteous in speech, and has abandoned slanderous speech—

181.

181.

Maccheravinaye yuttaṃ, saccaṃ kodhābhibhuṃ naraṃ;

Taṃ ve devā tāvatiṃsā, āhu “sappuriso”iti.

One devoted to dispelling avarice, truthful, a person who conquers anger—such a one the Tāvatiṃsa gods declare to be a 'true person'.

182.

182.

Kulajāto kulaputto, kulavaṃsasurakkhato;

Attanā dukkhappattopi, hīnakammaṃ na kāraye.

A son of good family, of noble birth, who carefully guards his family's lineage, even when afflicted by suffering, would never do a base deed.

183.

Sītalaṃ yadi naraggi, pabbatagge ca uppalaṃ;

Vikase na viparitā, sādhuvācā kudācanaṃ.

The sun might rise in the west, Mount Meru might bend, fire might become cool, and a lotus might bloom on a mountain peak; but the word of the good is never otherwise.

184.

Tato ācerassa rañño, tato buddhassanekadhā.

Pleasant is the shade of a tree; more pleasant is the shade of kinsmen, mother, and father. More pleasant still is the shade of a teacher and a king; but in many ways, the shade of the Buddha is most pleasant of all.

185.

Bhamarā [M 0.0111] pupphamicchanti, guṇamicchanti sajjanā;

Makkhikā pūtimicchanti, dosamicchanti dujjanā.

Bees desire flowers, the virtuous desire goodness; flies desire what is putrid, the wicked desire faults.

186.

Ubho mātupituhīnā, dubbhāsā ca dukkiriyā.

One without a mother is ill-spoken; one without a father is an evildoer. One without both mother and father is ill-spoken and an evildoer.

187.

Ubhomātu pituseṭṭhā, subhāsā ca sukiriyā.

One who esteems his mother is well-spoken; one who esteems his father is a doer of good deeds. One who esteems both mother and father is well-spoken and a doer of good deeds.

188.

Sunakho [M 0.0113] sunakhaṃ disvā, dantaṃ dasseti hiṃsituṃ;

Dujjano sujanaṃ disvā, rosayaṃ hiṃsamicchati.

A dog, seeing another dog, bares its teeth, intending to harm. So too, a wicked person, seeing a good person, becomes angry and wishes to harm.

189.

Sahasā kāritaṃ kammaṃ, bālo pacchānutappati.

Deeds should never be done in haste; a fool comes to regret a deed done rashly.

190.

Kodhaṃ [M 0.0114] vadhitvā na kadāci socati,

Makkhappahānaṃ isayo vaṇṇayanti;

Sabbesaṃ vuttaṃ pharusaṃ khametha,

Etaṃ khantiṃ uttamamāhu santo.

Having slain anger, one never grieves; the seers praise the abandonment of denigration. One should endure all harsh words; this the good call the supreme patience.

191.

191.

Dukkho nivāso sambādhe, ṭhāne asucīsaṅkate;

Tato arimhi appiye, tatopi akataññunā.

Painful is dwelling in a crowded place, in a place that is impure and perilous. More painful is dwelling with an enemy or one who is unloved; and even more painful is dwelling with an ungrateful person.

192.

192.

Ovadeyyā’nusāseyya[M 0.0115], asabbhā ca nivāraye;

Satañhi so piyo hoti, asataṃ hotiappiyo.

One should advise and instruct, and restrain others from what is base. For such a one is dear to the good, but displeasing to the wicked.

193.

193.

Uttamattanivātena, kakkhaḷaṃ mudunā jaye;

Nīcaṃ appakadānena, vāyāmena samaṃ jaye.

Conquer the superior with humility, the harsh with gentleness; conquer the mean with a small gift, and the equal with effort.

194.

194.

Na [M 0.0116] visaṃ visamiccāha, dhanaṃ saṅghassa uccate;

Visaṃ ekaṃva hanati, hanati saṅghassa sabbaṃ.

Poison is not the real poison, they say; the property of the Sangha is called poison. Poison slays only one person; the Sangha's property destroys all.

195.

195.

Dhanamappampi [M 0.0117] sādhūnaṃ, kūpe vāriva nissayo;

Bahuṃapi asādhūnaṃ, na ca vāriva aṇṇave.

Even a little wealth of the good is a support, like water in a well; but even the great wealth of the wicked is no support, like the water in the ocean.

196.

196.

Apattheyyaṃ na pattheyya, acinteyyaṃ na cintaye;

Dhammameva sucinteyya, kālaṃ moghaṃ na icchaye.

One should not desire what is not to be desired, nor ponder what is not to be pondered. One should reflect well only on the Dhamma and not wish for one's time to be spent in vain.

197.

197.

Acintitampi [M 0.0118] bhavati, cintitampi vinassati;

Na hi cintāmayā bhogā, itthiyā purisassa vā.

Even what is unthought of comes to be, and what is thought of perishes; for possessions do not arise from thought, for a woman or for a man.

198.

198.

Asantassa piyo hoti, sante na kurute piyaṃ;

Asataṃ dhammaṃ roceti, taṃ parābhavato mukhaṃ.

He to whom the wicked are dear, who holds no affection for the good, and who approves of the teaching of the wicked—this is a cause of his downfall.

199.

199.

Guṇā [M 0.0119] kubbanti dūtattaṃ, dūrepi vasataṃ sataṃ;

Ketake gandhaṃ ghāyitvā, gacchanti bhamarā sayaṃ.

Virtues act as messengers for the good, even when they dwell afar; smelling the fragrance of the ketaka flower, bees go there of their own accord.

200.

200.

Pubbajātikataṃ kammaṃ, taṃ kammamīti kathyate;

Tasmā purisākārenaṃ, yataṃ kare atandito.

A deed done in a past life is called 'kamma'; therefore, one should strive tirelessly with human effort.

201.

201.

Mattikapiṇḍato kattā, kurute yaṃ yadicchati;

Evamattakataṃ kammaṃ, māṇavo paṭipajjate.

As a potter from a lump of clay makes whatever he wishes, so a person experiences the kamma made by himself.

202.

202.

Uṭṭhāyoṭṭhāya [M 0.0121] bodheyyaṃ, mahabbhaya mupaṭṭhitaṃ;

Maraṇabyādhisokānaṃ, kimajja nipatissati.

Rise up, rise up! One should be awake, for a great danger is present. Of death, sickness, and sorrow, what will befall today?

203.

203.

Pāṇā yathāttanobhiṭṭhā, bhūtānamapi te tathā;

Attopamena bhūtesu, dayaṃ kubbanti sādhavo.

Just as life is dear to oneself, so it is to other beings. Making oneself the example, the good show compassion for all beings.

Sabbe [M 0.0122] tasanti daṇḍassa, sabbe bhāyanti maccuno;

Attānaṃ upamaṃ katvā, na haneyya na ghātaye.

All tremble at the rod, all fear death. Making oneself the comparison, one should neither kill nor cause to kill.

204.

204.

Bālo vā yadi vā vuddho, yuvā vā gehamāgato;

Tassa pūjā vidhātabbā, sabbassābhyāgato garu.

Whether a child, an elder, or a youth has come to the house, honor should be paid to him; for to everyone, a guest is venerable.

205.

205.

Ākiṇṇopi asantehi, asaṃsaṭṭhova bhaddako;

Bahunā sannajātena, gacchena ubbattenidha.

Even when crowded by the base, the good person remains unmingled, like one pulled out from a great entangled thicket.

Bāladujjanakaṇḍa

The Chapter on Fools and the Wicked

206.

206.

Kāyaduccaritādīhi[M 0.0124], sampanno pāpamānujo;

Bāloti lokanāthena, kittito dhammasāminā.

Endowed with bodily misconduct and so forth, that sinful man is proclaimed a fool by the Lord of the World, the Master of the Dhamma.

207.

207.

Ducintitacintī ceva, dubbhāsitabhāsīpi ca;

Dukkaṭakammakārī ca, pāpako bālamānujo.

One who thinks evil thoughts, speaks evil words, and does evil deeds—such a wicked person is a fool.

208.

208.

Atipiyo [M 0.0125] na kātabbo, khalo kotuhalaṃ karo;

Sirasā vahamānopi, aḍḍhapūro ghaṭo yathā.

A meddlesome rogue should not be held too dear; even when carried on the head, he is like a half-filled pot.

209.

209.

Sappo duṭṭho khalo duṭṭho, sappā duṭṭhataro khalo;

Mantosadhehi taṃ sappaṃ, khalaṃ kenupasammati.

A snake is venomous, a rogue is malicious; the rogue is more malicious than the snake. A snake may be subdued by mantras and herbs, but by what can a rogue be pacified?

210.

210.

Yo [M 0.0126] bālo maññati bālyaṃ, paṇḍito vāpi tenaso;

Bālo ca paṇḍitamānī, sa ve bāloti vuccati.

A fool who recognizes his foolishness is wise at least to that extent; but a fool who thinks himself wise is truly called a fool.

211.

211.

Madhūva maññati bālo, yāva pāpaṃ na paccati;

Yadā ca paccati pāpaṃ, atha dukkhaṃ nigacchati.

The fool thinks it is like honey, so long as evil does not ripen; but when evil ripens, then the fool comes to suffering.

212.

212.

Na [M 0.0127] sādhu balavā bālo, sāhasā vindate dhanaṃ;

Kāyassa bhedā duppañño, nirayaṃ sopapajjati.

A fool who is strong is not good, for he gains wealth by violence; at the body's dissolution, the unwise one is reborn in hell.

213.

213.

Ghare [M 0.0128] duṭṭho mūsiko ca, vane duṭṭho ca vānaro;

Sakuṇe ca duṭṭho kāko, nareduṭṭho ca brāhmaṇo.

In the house, the wicked one is the mouse; in the forest, the wicked one is the monkey; among birds, the wicked one is the crow; among men, the wicked one is the Brahmin.

214.

214.

Dīghā jāgarato ratti, dīghaṃ santassa yojanaṃ;

Dīgho bālānasaṃsāro, saddhammaṃ avijānataṃ.

Long is the night for one who is awake; long is a league for one who is weary; long is saṃsāra for fools who do not know the true Dhamma.

215.

215.

Tilamattaṃ [M 0.0129] paresañca, appadosañca passati;

Nāḷikerampi sadosaṃ, khalajāto na passati.

He sees the fault of others, though as small as a sesame seed, but does not see his own fault, though as large as a coconut. Such is the rogue.

216.

216.

Nattadosaṃ pare jaññā, jaññā dosaṃ parassatu;

Guyho kummāva aṅgāni, paradosañca lakkhaye.

One may know the fault of another, but not one's own. Hiding one's own faults like a tortoise concealing its limbs, one observes the faults of others.

217.

217.

Luddhaṃ [M 0.0130] atthena gaṇheyya, thaddhaṃ añjalikammunā;

Chandānuvattiyā mūḷhaṃ, yathābhūtena paṇḍitaṃ.

One should win over the greedy with a gift, the stubborn with folded hands, the fool with compliance, and the wise with the truth.

218.

218.

Yathā udumbarapakkā, bahi rattakā eva ca;

Antokimila sampuṇṇā, evaṃ dujjanahadayā.

Just as the ripe udumbara fruit is red on the outside but full of worms within, so are the hearts of wicked people.

219.

219.

Yāvajīvampi [M 0.0131] ce bālo, paṇḍitaṃ payirupāsati;

Na so dhammaṃ vijānāti, dabbī sūparasaṃ yathā.

Even if a fool attends upon a wise person for his whole life, he does not understand the Dhamma, just as a ladle does not know the taste of soup.

220.

220.

Carañce nādhigaccheyya, seyyaṃ sadisamattano;

Ekacariyaṃ daḷhaṃ kayirā, natthi bāle sahāyatā.

If, in one's journey, one does not find a companion who is better or equal to oneself, let one resolutely pursue a solitary course; there is no fellowship with a fool.

221.

221.

Ajātamatamūḷhānaṃ[M 0.0132], varamādayo na cantimo;

Sakiṃ dukkhakarāvāda-yontimo tu pade pade.

Of the unborn, the dead, and the fool, the first two are better, not the last. The former cause suffering but once, while the latter does so at every step.

222.

222.

Dujjanena [M 0.0133] samaṃ veraṃ, sakhyañcāpi na kāraye;

Uṇho dahati caṅgāro, sīto kaṇhāyate karaṃ.

With a wicked person, one should cultivate neither enmity nor friendship; a hot ember burns, while a cold one blackens the hand.

223.

223.

Dujjano piyavādī ca, netaṃ vissāsakāraṇaṃ;

Madhu tiṭṭhati jivhagge, hadaye halāhalaṃ visaṃ.

A wicked person who speaks pleasantly is no cause for trust; honey is on the tip of his tongue, but deadly poison is in his heart.

224.

224.

Dujjano [M 0.0134] parihātabbo, vijjāyālaṅkatopi ca;

Maṇinā bhūsito sappo, kimeso na bhayaṅkaro.

The wicked should be avoided, even if adorned with learning. A snake adorned with a jewel—is it not fearsome?

225.

225.

Nāḷikerasamākārā, dissantepi hi sajjanā;

Aññe badarikākārā, bahireva manoharā.

Good people are indeed like coconuts; others are like jujube fruits, charming only on the outside.

Yathāpi [M 0.0135] pana sapakkā, bahi kaṇṭakameva ca;

Anto amatasampuṇṇā, evaṃ sujanahadayā.

Just as a ripe jackfruit, though thorny on the outside, is filled with nectar within, so too are the hearts of good people.

Yathā udumbarapakkā, bahi rattakameva ca;

Evaṃ kimilasampuṇā, evaṃ dujjanahadayā.

Just as the ripe udumbara fruit is red on the outside, and so full of worms, so are the hearts of wicked people.

226.

226.

Dosabhīto anārambho, taṃ kā purisalakkhaṇaṃ;

Kohyajiṇṇabhayā nanu, bhojanaṃ parihīyate.

Fearful of fault, not initiating action—what kind of man's characteristic is that? Who indeed, fearing indigestion, would abstain from food?

227.

227.

Payopānaṃ bhujaṅgānaṃ, kevalaṃ visavaḍḍhanaṃ;

Upadeso hi mūḷhānaṃ, pakopāya na santiyā.

Giving milk to serpents only increases their venom; likewise, advice to fools leads to provocation, not to peace.

228.

228.

Na [M 0.0136] ṭhātabbaṃ na gantabbaṃ, dujjanena samaṃ kvaci;

Dujjano hi dukkhaṃ deti, na so sukhaṃ kadācipi.

One should neither stand nor go anywhere with a wicked person; for the wicked person gives suffering, and never happiness.

229.

229.

Abaddhā tattha bajjhanti, yattha bālā pabhāsare;

Baddhāpi tattha muccanti, yattha dhīrā pabhāsare.

The unbound are bound where fools hold forth; even the bound are freed where the wise hold forth.

230.

230.

Parittaṃ [M 0.0137] dārumāruyha, yathā sīde mahaṇṇave;

Evaṃ kusitamāgamma, sādhu jīvipi sīdati;

Tasmā taṃ parivajjeyya, kusītaṃ hīnavīriyaṃ.

Just as one who has mounted a small piece of wood would sink in the great ocean, so too, by associating with the lazy, even one of good livelihood sinks. Therefore, one should avoid that lazy person of weak effort.

231.

231.

Saddhāsīlādisampanno[M 0.0138], sumitto sādhumānuso;

Tādisaṃ mittaṃ seveyya, vuddhikāmo vicakkhaṇo.

A good friend, a virtuous person, endowed with faith, virtue, and the like—a discerning person desiring growth should associate with such a friend.

232.

232.

Dānādiguṇaseṭṭhehi, miditabbo mitto hi kho;

Tādisaṃ avaṅkeneva, manasā bhajeyya sudhī.

A friend should indeed be cultivated through generosity and other superior qualities; a wise person should associate with such a one with a sincere mind.

333.

333.

Hitakkaro [M 0.0139] paro bandhu, bandhupi ahito paro;

Ahito dehajo byādhi, hitaṃ araññamosadhaṃ.

A benefactor, though a stranger, is a kinsman; a kinsman, if harmful, is a stranger. A harmful person is like a disease born of the body; a helpful thing is like a medicine from the forest.

234.

234.

Parokkhe [M 0.0140] kiccahantāraṃ, paccakkhe piyavādinaṃ;

Vajjaye tādisaṃ mittaṃ, visakumbhaṃ payomukhaṃ.

One should avoid a friend who ruins one's affairs behind one's back but speaks sweetly to one's face; such a one is like a pot of poison with milk on its rim.

135.

135.

Dhanahīne caje mitto, puttadārā sahodarā;

Dhanavanteva sevanti, dhanaṃ loke mahāsakhā.

When one is without wealth, friends, wife, children, and siblings forsake him. They serve only the wealthy; wealth is the greatest friend in the world.

236.

236.

Jāniyā [M 0.0141] pesane bhacce, bandhave byasanāgame;

Mittañca āpadikāle, bhariyañca vibhavakkhaye.

One may know a servant by an errand, kinsmen when misfortune comes, a friend in a time of adversity, and a wife when wealth is lost.

237.

237.

Ussave byasane ceva, dubbhikkhe sattuviggahe;

Rājadvāre susāne ca, yo tiṭṭhati so bandhavā.

At festivals and in calamities, in famine and in war with enemies, at the king’s gate and at the cremation ground—whoever stands by one, that person is a kinsman.

238.

238.

Na [M 0.0142]2 vissase amittassa, mittañcāpi na vissase;

Kadāci kupite mitte, sabbadosaṃ pakāsati.

Do not trust an enemy, nor even trust a friend; for should a friend become angry, he will expose all your faults.

239.

239.

Mātā mittaṃ pitā ceti, sabhāvā taṃ tayaṃ hitaṃ;

Kammakaraṇato caññe, bhavanti hitabuddhiyo.

Mother is a friend, and so is father; these three are beneficial by nature. Others, by their actions, become well-wishers.

240.

240.

Āpadāsu [M 0.0143] mittaṃ jaññā, yuddhe sūraṃ iṇe suciṃ;

Bhariyaṃ khīṇesu vittesu, byasanesu ca bandhavaṃ.

One should know a friend in adversity, a hero in battle, an honest person in debt, a wife when wealth is exhausted, and a kinsman in misfortune.

241.

241.

Sakiṃ duṭṭhañca yo mittaṃ, puna sandhātumicchati;

Sa maccumupagaṇhāti, gabbhaṃ assatarī yathā.

One who seeks to reconcile with a friend who once became hostile, takes up death, just as a she-mule does by conceiving.

242.

242.

Iṇaseso [M 0.0144] aggiseso, rogaseso tatheva ca;

Punappunaṃ vivaḍḍhanti, tasmā sesaṃ na kāraye.

A remnant of debt, a remnant of fire, and likewise a remnant of disease, repeatedly increase; therefore, one should leave no remainder.

243.

243.

Padumaṃva mukhaṃ yassa, vācā candanasītalā;

Tādisaṃ no paseveyya, hadaye tu halāhalaṃ.

One whose face is like a lotus, whose speech is as cool as sandalwood, but whose heart holds deadly poison—one should not associate with such a person.

244.

244.

Na [M 0.0145] seve pharusaṃ sāmiṃ, na ca seveyya macchariṃ;

Tato apaggaṇha sāmiṃ, neva niggahitaṃ tato.

Do not serve a harsh master, nor a miserly one. Withdraw from such a master, without being reproached by him.

245.

245.

Kudesañca [M 0.0146] kumittañca, kukulañca kubandhavaṃ;

Kudārañca kudāsañca, dūrato parivajjaye.

A bad country, a bad friend, a bad family, and bad relatives; a bad wife and a bad servant—these one should avoid from afar.

246.

246.

Sītavāco bahumitto, pharuso appamittako;

Upamā ettha ñātabbā, sūriyacandarājūnaṃ.

One of gentle speech has many friends; a harsh person has few. The simile for this should be understood as that of the sun and the moon.

247.

247.

Ahitā paṭisedho ca, hitesu ca payojanaṃ;

Byasane apariccāgo, itidaṃ mittalakkhaṇaṃ.

The prevention of what is harmful, engagement in what is beneficial, and not forsaking in misfortune—this is the mark of a friend.

248.

248.

Piyo [M 0.0147] garu bhāvanīyo, vattā ca vacanakkhamo;

Gambhīrañca kathaṃ kattā, no caṭṭhāne niyojako;

Tādisaṃ mittaṃ seveyya, bhūtikāmo vicakkhaṇo.

Lovable, respectable, worthy of esteem; a speaker patient with rebuke; one who speaks profound counsel and does not engage one in wrong pursuits—such a friend should be cultivated by a discerning person who desires prosperity.

Piyo [M 0.0148] garu bhāva nīyo, vattā ca vacanakkhamo;

Gambhirañca kathaṃ kattā, no caṭṭhāne niyojako.

Lovable, respectable, worthy of esteem; a speaker patient with rebuke; one who speaks profound counsel and does not engage one in wrong pursuits.

Yamhi etāni ṭhānāni, saṃvijjantidha puggale;

So mitto mittakāmena, atthakāmānukampato;

Api nāsiyamānena, bhajitabbo tathāvidho.

The person in whom these qualities are found here—such a friend should be associated with by one who desires friendship, for the sake of welfare and out of compassion, even while perishing.

249.

249.

Orasaṃ katasambandhaṃ, tathā vaṃsakkamāgataṃ;

Rakkhako byasanehi, mittaṃ ñeyyaṃ catubbidhaṃ.

A friend should be known as fourfold: a kinsman, one who has formed a bond, one who is a hereditary friend, and a protector in times of misfortune.

250.

250.

Piyo garu bhāvaniyo, vattā ca vacanakkhamo;

Gambhirañca kathaṃ kattā, na caṭṭhāne niyojako;

Taṃ mittaṃ mittakāmena, yāvajīvampi seviyaṃ.

Lovable, respectable, worthy of esteem; a speaker patient with rebuke; one who speaks profound counsel and does not engage one in wrong pursuits—such a friend should be served for life by one who desires friendship.

Piyo garu bhāvanīyo, vattā ca vacanakkhamo;

Gambhirañca kathaṃ kattā, no caṭṭhāne niyojako;

Tādisaṃ mittaṃ seveyya, bhūtikāmo vicakkhaṇo.

Lovable, respectable, worthy of esteem; a speaker patient with rebuke; one who speaks profound counsel and does not engage one in wrong pursuits—such a friend should be cultivated by a discerning person who desires prosperity.

Rājakaṇḍa

The Section on Kings

251.

251.

Mahājanaṃ [M 0.0150] yo rañjeti, catūhipi vatthūhi vā;

Rājāti vuccate loke, iti sallakkhaye vidvā.

He who gratifies the great populace with the four bases of beneficence is called a 'king' in the world; thus should the wise one discern.

252.

252.

Dānañca atthacariyā, piyavācā attasamaṃ;

Saṅgahā caturo ime, munindena pakāsitā.

Generosity, helpful conduct, endearing speech, and impartiality: these four means of beneficence have been proclaimed by the Chief of Sages.

Dānampi [M 0.0151] atthacariyatañca, piyavāditañca samānattatañca;

Kariyacariyasusaṅgahaṃ bahūnaṃ, anavamatena guṇena yāti saggaṃ;

Generosity, helpful conduct, endearing speech, and impartiality; through this good beneficence to many, through this unblemished virtue, one goes to heaven.

Dānañca peyyavajjañca, atthacariyā ca yā idha;

Samānattatā ca dhammesu, tattha tattha yathārahaṃ;

Ete kho saṅgahā loke, rathassāṇīva yāyato.

Generosity and endearing speech, and helpful conduct here, and impartiality in all matters, in each case as is fit; these are the means of beneficence in the world, like the linchpin of a moving chariot.

253.

253.

Dānaṃ sīlaṃ pariccāgaṃ, ajjavaṃ maddavaṃ tapaṃ;

Akkodhaṃ avihiṃsañca, khantī ca avirodhanaṃ;

Dasete dhamme rājāno, appamattena dhārayyuṃ.

Generosity, virtue, liberality, rectitude, gentleness, austerity, non-anger, non-violence, patience, and non-opposition. Kings should diligently uphold these ten principles.

254.

254.

Ekayāmaṃ [M 0.0152] saye rājā, dviyā maññeva paṇḍito;

Gharāvāso tiyāmova, catuyāmo tu yācako.

A king should sleep for one watch, a wise person for two; a householder for three watches, and a beggar for four.

255.

255.

Aputtakaṃ gharaṃ suññaṃ, raṭṭhaṃ suññaṃ arājakaṃ;

Asippassa mukhaṃ suññaṃ, sabbasuññaṃ daliddattaṃ.

A childless home is empty; a kingless kingdom is empty; the mouth of an unlearned person is empty; but poverty is complete emptiness.

256.

256.

Dhanamicche [M 0.0153] vāṇijeyya, vijjamicche bhaje sutaṃ;

Puttamicche taruṇitthiṃ, rājāmaccaṃ icchāgate.

One who desires wealth should engage in trade; one who desires knowledge should attend on the learned; one who desires a son should seek a young woman; one who desires to be a king's minister should approach the king.

257.

257.

Pakkhīnaṃ balamākāso, macchānamudakaṃ balaṃ;

Dubbalassa balaṃ rājā, kumārānaṃ rudaṃ balaṃ.

For birds, the sky is their strength; for fish, water is their strength; for the weak, the king is their strength; for children, crying is their strength.

258.

258.

Khamā [M 0.0154] jāgariyuṭṭhānaṃ, saṃvibhāgo dayikkhaṇā;

Nāyakassa guṇā ete, icchitabbā sataṃ sadā.

Forbearance, vigilance and exertion, sharing, and compassionate regard: these are the qualities of a leader, always to be wished for by the good.

259.

259.

Sakiṃ vadanti rājāno, sakiṃ samaṇabrāhmaṇā;

Sakiṃ sappurisā loke, esa dhammo sanantano.

Kings speak but once; recluses and brahmins but once; good people in the world speak but once: this is the eternal law.

260.

260.

Alaso gīhi kāmabhogī na sādhu,

Asaññato pabbajito na sādhu;

Rājā na sādhu anisammakārī,

Yo paṇḍito kodhano taṃ na sādhu.

A lazy householder enjoying sensual pleasures is not good; an unrestrained renunciant is not good; a king who acts without due consideration is not good; a wise person who is angry—that is not good.

261.

261.

Āyaṃ khayaṃ sayaṃ jaññā, katākataṃ sayaṃ jaññā;

Niggahe niggahārahaṃ, paggahe paggahārahaṃ.

A kingdom is like a great tree. He who rules it righteously knows its flavor, and his kingdom does not perish.

A discontented ascetic is ruined, and a contented householder is ruined; a modest courtesan is ruined, and a shameless woman of good family is ruined.

Among these thievish women of many wiles, in whom truth is very hard to find, the nature of women is hard to know, like the path of a fish in the water.

Having seen conflict as a danger, and non-conflict as security; be united and courteous—this is the instruction of the Buddhas.

Having seen heedlessness as a danger, and heedfulness as security; cultivate the Eightfold Path—this is the instruction of the Buddhas.

Appakācekadesāva, na tā ikkheyya paṇḍito;

Dhammādhammaṃva ikkheyya, atthānatthaṃ hitāhitaṃ.

Blame and praise are impermanent and temporary, insignificant and partial. A wise person should not heed them, but should instead discern Dhamma and not-Dhamma, benefit and harm, the wholesome and the unwholesome.

Paṇāma paṭiññā

Homage and Declaration

Vatthuttayaṃ [M 0.0212] namasitvā, ācere kavipuṅgave;

Kassaṃ dvādasamāsānaṃ, bandhaṃ tammāsavasikaṃ.

Having paid homage to the Triple Gem and to the teachers, chief among poets, I will compose a work concerning the twelve months, dealing with the observances appropriate for each month.

1.

1.

Cittasammatamāso [M 0.0213] hi, atevaññehi sobhati;

Rammakamāso rammamāso, teneva vohāro bhavi.

Indeed, the month called Citta excels among others; it is the delightful month, the delightful month; hence, that is the term used.

2.

2.

Tasmiṃ sucittamāsamhi, nāgadumā supupphare;

Pupphanti asanadumā, vāyanti kānane hi ve.

In that beautiful month of Citta, the nāga trees are in full bloom; the asana trees blossom, and indeed the winds blow in the forest.

3.

3.

Saṅkanta mahussavopi, tamhi māsamhi vattate;

Gandhodakehi aññoññaṃ, siñcamānā sumodare.

The great festival of Saṅkanta also occurs in that month; sprinkling each other with fragrant water, they greatly rejoice.

Yathāpi [M 0.0214] rammako māso, gimhānaṃ hoti brāhmaṇa;

Ate‘va’ññehi māsehi, dumapupphehi sobhati.

Just as the delightful month, O brahmin, is of the summer, so it excels other months, shining with tree blossoms.

Sambuddho cittamāsassa, kāḷapakkhe uposathe;

Pātoyeva samādāya, pavaraṃ pattacīvaraṃ;

Anukampāya nāgānaṃ, nāgadīpamupāgami.

The Fully Enlightened One, in the month of Citta, on the Uposatha of the dark fortnight, having taken up his excellent bowl and robe in the morning, out of compassion for the nāgas, approached Nāgadīpa.

4.

4.

Vesākhavhayamāso tu, suvisiṭṭho supākaṭo;

Lokagganāthaṃ paṭicca, santehi abhilakkhito.

Indeed, the month called Vesākha is truly distinguished and well-known, marked by the peaceful ones on account of the Lord of the World.

5.

5.

Tamhi [M 0.0215] vesākhamāsamhi, campakāpi supupphare;

Bodhiṃ dakehi siñcitvā, sajjanā sampamodare.

In that month of Vesākha, even the campaka trees bloomed well; having watered the Bodhi tree with water, the virtuous rejoiced together.

6.

6.

Vanesuva potakāpi, pakkhandanti disodisaṃ;

Vikūjantā sabhāsāya, janasotarasāyanaṃ.

Even young birds in the woods dart in every direction, singing sweetly with their own voices, a delight to the ears of the people.

Dutiye [M 0.0216] divase bhatta-kāle ārocite jino;

Ramme vesākhamāsamhi, puṇṇāmāyaṃ munissaro.

On the second day, when mealtime was announced, the Victor, the Sage-Lord, in the delightful month of Vesākha, on the full moon day...

7.

7.

Jeṭṭhasammatamāsopi, sogatajanabbhantare;

Vikhyāto lakkhañño ceva, jeṭṭhena saṃyuto hi ve.

Even the month called Jeṭṭha is renowned within the community of the Well-Gone One's followers; and indeed it is auspicious and connected with the chief star, Jeṭṭha.

8.

8.

Tasmiṃhi jeṭṭhamāsamhi, sumanā vanamallikā;

Pupphanti ca pavāyanti, sabbajanamanoharā.

In that month of Jeṭṭha, the jasmine and wild jasmine flowers bloom and spread fragrance, charming the minds of all people.

9.

9.

Parikkhaṇāsusabhāpi, abhavi mranamāmaṇḍale;

Khetale jeṭṭhajotipi, pajjali tasmiñhi ve.

Even an assembly for examination occurred in the Myanmar region; and indeed the Jeṭṭha light blazed in the field there.

10.

10.

Āsāḷho nāma māsopi, atīva visiṭṭho bhavi;

Paṭisandhiṃ gaṇhi buddho, tasmiñhi muni sudhī.

The month called Āsāḷha was also exceedingly distinguished; in that month the wise Sage, the Buddha, took conception.

11.

Nikkhamipi ca sambuddho, dhammacakkaṃ pavattayi;

Upasampadakammampi, karonti tasmiṃpi hi.

And the Fully Enlightened One also went forth and set in motion the Wheel of Dhamma; indeed, the act of higher ordination is also performed in that month.

12.

Punnāgadumā pupphanti, pavāyanti disodisaṃ;

Ādicco tiṭṭhati tamhi, uttarāyānakoṭiyaṃ.

The Punnāga trees are flowering, shedding fragrance in every direction; the sun stands there at the peak of its northern journey.

Sīhe [M 0.0219] sāvaṇamāsamhi, salākadānamuttamaṃ;

Denti sādhavo mānusā, saddahantā vatthuttayaṃ.

In the month of Sāvaṇa, when the sun is in Leo, the ticket-offering is supreme; virtuous people give, having faith in the Triple Gem.

Khe savaṇanakkhattampi, atīva jotayī hi ve.

Even the Kaṭeruha flowers bloom in that month of Sāvaṇa; and indeed the constellation of Savaṇa shone exceedingly bright in the sky.

Māse vācanauggaṇha-kammaṃkaṃsu sukhāsayā.

And during the rainy season, the recluses, spiritual sons of the Well-Gone One, happily undertook the work of recitation and learning in that month.

Nadīsu dakapūritā, kaṭapatthatasādisā.

In the beautiful month of Poṭṭhapāda, associated with the sign of Virgo, the rivers were filled with water, resembling spread-out mats.

People then hold great boat festivals; men and women in the kingdom play and rejoice.

Indeed, even the golden Yamaduka trees blossom then; the cloud rains a little, a little, and frost also falls.

In the rainy month of Assayuja, various delightful, water-born flowers, such as lotuses, indeed bloom.

Pūjenti lokagganāthaṃ, sādhavo sogatājanā.

With rows of great lamps, upon the entire land of Myanmar, the virtuous followers of the Sugata worship the Foremost Lord of the World.

The ascetics, rich in austerity, having completed the rains-retreat, wander in all directions; like the virtuous who delight in giving, they become cool and are happy.

In the month of Māgasira, in the sign of Dhanu, considered the winter season, the excellent worship called Sattidhara and an assembly also took place.

In the month of Phussa, in the sign of Makara, the flowers of the beautiful blue creepers bloom and spread their fragrance, enchanting the minds of the people.

The kings of the Myanmar region also arrange military parades; with their retinues, they watch the elephants, horses, and so forth.

At the peak of the southern solstice, on the full moon day of Aṭṭha, although it is extremely cold at that time, the sun is honored by the world.

Laṃkādīpaṃ visodhetuṃ, laṅkādīpamupāgami.

In the ninth month after his enlightenment, on the full moon day of Phussa, the Victor came to the island of Lanka in order to purify it.

Pumtālā madhurarasaṃ, mānujānaṃ dadanti ca.

In the month of Māgha, in the sign of Kumbha, the cotton trees bloom beautifully; and the male palmyra palms give sweet juice to the people.

A great festival of gruel is also manifest on the land of Myanmar; and the remaining clouds, thundering, rain down.

In the month of Phagguna, in the sign of Mīna, beautiful and fragrant trees bloom in the forest, resplendent with new leaves.

At that time, the wind indeed blows from the southern direction; having made stupas of sand on the sandy ground, they worship them.

In the first month of summer, various trees bloom profusely; thus, the entire forest becomes variegated and truly delightful to behold.
