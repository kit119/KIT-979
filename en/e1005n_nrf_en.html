Namo tassa bhagavato arahato sammāsambuddhassa

Homage to the Blessed One, the Arahant, the Perfectly Self-Enlightened One.

Lokanīti

Worldly Wisdom

1. Paṇḍitakaṇḍo

1. The Chapter on the Wise

1.

1.

Lokanītiṃ [M 0.0001] pavakkhāmi,

Nānāsatthasamuddhaṭaṃ;

Māgadheneva saṅkhepaṃ,

Vanditvā ratanatthayaṃ.

I shall declare the Lokanīti, extracted from various treatises, concisely in the Magadhan language, having paid homage to the Triple Gem.

2.

2.

Nītiloke purisassa sāro,

Mātā pitā ācariyoca mitto;

Tasmā hi nītiṃ puriso vijaññā,

Ñāṇī mahā hoti bahussutoca.

In the world of policy, the essence of a person is mother, father, teacher, and friend. Therefore, a person should understand policy; the wise and very learned become great.

3.

3.

Alasassa kuto sippaṃ,

Asippassa kuto dhanaṃ;

Adhanassa kuto mittaṃ,

Amittassa kuto sukhaṃ;

Asukhassa [M 0.0002] kuto puññaṃ,

Apuññassa kuto varaṃ.

Whence skill for the lazy? Whence wealth for the unskilled? Whence friends for the penniless? Whence happiness for the friendless? Whence merit for the unhappy? Whence excellence for the meritless?

4.

4.

Sippā samaṃ dhanaṃ natthi,

Sippaṃ corā nagaṇhare;

Idha loke sippaṃ mittaṃ,

Paraloke sukhāvahaṃ.

There is no wealth equal to skill; thieves cannot take skill. In this world, skill is a friend, and in the next world, it brings happiness.

5.

5.

Appakaṃ nātimaññeyya,

Citte sutaṃ nidhāpaye;

Vammikodakabindūva,

Cirena paripūrati.

One should not despise what is little; one should store what is learned in the mind. Like a water-pot with drops of water, in time it becomes full.

6.

6.

Khuddoti nātimaññeyya,

Vijjaṃ vā sippameva vā;

Ekampi pariyodātaṃ,

Jīvitakappakāraṇaṃ.

One should not despise it as small, whether it be knowledge or skill. Even a single one, when perfected, can be a means for a livelihood.

7.

7.

Sele sele na māṇikaṃ,

Gaje gaje na muttikaṃ;

Vane vane na candanaṃ,

Ṭhāne ṭhāne na paṇḍitaṃ.

On every mountain there is not a gem, in every elephant there is not a pearl, in every forest there is not sandalwood, in every place there is not a wise person.

8.

8.

Paṇḍito sutasampanno,

Yattha atthīti ce suto;

Mahussāhena [M 0.0003] taṃ ṭhānaṃ,

Gantabbaṃva sutesinā.

A wise person, endowed with learning—wherever it is heard that such a one exists, to that place a seeker of learning should go with great effort.

9.

9.

Sine sippaṃ sine dhanaṃ,

Sine pabbatamāruhaṃ;

Sine kāmassa kodhassa,

Ime pañca sine sine.

Gradually acquire skill, gradually acquire wealth, gradually ascend a mountain, gradually overcome desire and anger—these five should be done gradually, gradually.

10.

10.

Suti sammuti saṅkhyāca,

Yogā nīti visesakā;

Gandhabbā gaṇikā ceva,

Dhanu bedā ca pūraṇā.

Revelation, conventional law, calculation, Yoga, statecraft, Vaisheshika philosophy, music, the arts of courtesans, archery, and the Puranas.

11.

11.

Tikicchā itihāsā ca,

Joti māyā ca chandati;

Ketu mantā ca saddā ca,

Sippāṭṭhārasakā ime.

Medicine, history, astrology, magic, prosody, the interpretation of omens, incantations, and grammar—these are the eighteen arts.

12.

12.

Apuṭṭho paṇḍito bherī,

Pajjunno me hoti pucchito;

Bālo puṭṭho apuṭṭhopi,

Bahumpi bhaṇate sadā.

Unasked, the wise one is like a drum; when asked, he is like Pajjunna, the thunder god. The fool, whether asked or unasked, always speaks much.

13.

13.

Potthakesu ca yaṃ sippaṃ,

Parahatthesu yaṃ dhanaṃ;

Yathākicce [M 0.0004] samuppanne,

Na taṃ sippaṃ na taṃ dhanaṃ.

The skill that is only in books, and the wealth that is in others' hands—when a need arises, that is not skill, nor is that wealth.

14.

14.

Jalappamāṇaṃ kumuddanālaṃ,

Kulappamāṇaṃ vinayo pamāṇaṃ;

Byattippamāṇaṃ kathītavākyaṃ,

Pathaviyā pamāṇaṃ tiṇa milātaṃ.

The measure of the water is the lotus stalk; the measure of a family is discipline; the measure of an eloquent person is their speech; the measure of the earth is the withered grass.

15.

15.

Appassuto sutaṃ appaṃ,

Bahuṃ maññati mānavā;

Sindhūdakaṃ apassanto,

Kūpe toyaṃva maṇḍuko.

A person of little learning considers his small learning to be much, like a frog in a well that has never seen the waters of the ocean.

16.

16.

Pathamaṃ parājaye sippaṃ,

Dutiyaṃ parājaye dhanaṃ;

Tatiyaṃ parājaye dhammaṃ,

Catutthaṃ kiṃ karissati.

In the first defeat, one loses skill; in the second, one loses wealth; in the third, one loses virtue. What will one do in the fourth?

17.

17.

Byatta putra kimalaso,

Abyatto bhārahārako;

Byattako pūjito loke,

Byatta putra dine dine.

Of what use is a skilled son if he is lazy? An unskilled one is a mere burden-bearer. A skilled person is honored in the world; a skilled son is honored day by day.

18.

18.

Mātā verī pitā satru,

Kena bāle na sikkhitā;

Sabhāmajjhe [M 0.0005] na sobhanti,

Haṃsamajjhe bakoyathā.

A mother is an enemy, a father is a foe, if they do not educate their child. Such a child does not shine in an assembly, just as a heron does not among swans.

19.

19.

Kaṇṭakaṃ giri ko tikkhati,

Ko añjanaṃ migakkhikaṃ;

Uppathaṃ pallale ko sugandhaṃ,

Kula-putta-rūpo ko pavattati;

Sāmaṃ-bhāvo.

Who sharpens the thorn on the mountain? Who applies collyrium to the deer's eyes? Who gives fragrance to the lotus in the marsh? Who makes the son of a good family excel? It is their own nature.

20.

20.

Na rasaṃ akotambulaṃ,

Adhanassa, laṅkatampi;

Alonakantu byañjanaṃ,

Byākaraṇaṃ asippassa.

There is no taste in unprepared betel; for the penniless, even adornment is tasteless; a curry without salt is without flavor; and so is grammar for the unlearned.

21.

21.

Sussusā sutasampanno,

Sutāpaññāya pavaḍḍhati;

Paññāya atthaṃ jānāti,

Ñāto attho sukhāvaho.

By desiring to listen, one becomes accomplished in learning; from learning, wisdom grows; through wisdom, one understands the meaning; the meaning, once understood, brings happiness.

22.

22.

Bhojanaṃ methunaṃ niddā,

Goṇe posepi vijjati;

Vijjā viseso posassa,

Taṃ hīno gosamo bhave.

Eating, sexual intercourse, and sleep are found in cattle as well as in humans. Knowledge is the special distinction of a human; one lacking it is the same as cattle.

23.

23.

Natthi vijjāsamaṃmittaṃ,

Naca byādhisamo ripu;

Naca [M 0.0006] attasamaṃ pemaṃ,

Naca kammasamaṃ balaṃ.

There is no friend equal to knowledge, no enemy equal to disease, no love equal to love for oneself, and no power equal to kamma.

24.

24.

Haṃso majjhe na kākānaṃ,

Sīho gunnaṃ na sobhate;

Gadrabhamajjhe turaṅgo,

Bālamajjhe ca paṇḍito.

A swan does not shine among crows, nor a lion among cattle; a horse does not shine among donkeys, nor a wise person among fools.

25.

25.

Yāvajīvampi ce bālo,

Paṇḍitaṃ payirupāsati;

Na so dhammaṃ vijānāti,

Dabbi sūparasaṃ yathā.

Even if a fool attends upon a wise person all his life, he does not understand the Dhamma, just as a ladle does not know the taste of soup.

26.

26.

Muhuttamapi ce viññū,

Paṇḍitaṃ payirupāsati;

Khippaṃ dhammaṃ vijānāti,

Jivhā sūparasaṃ yathā.

If an intelligent person associates with a wise one even for a moment, he quickly understands the Dhamma, just as the tongue knows the taste of soup.

27.

27.

Vinā satthaṃ na gaccheyya,

Sūro saṅgāmabhūmiyaṃ;

Paṇḍitvāddhagū vāṇijo,

Videsagamano tathā.

A hero should not go to the battlefield without a weapon; likewise, a wise person, a traveler, a merchant, or one going to a foreign land should not go unprepared.

28.

28.

Dhananāsaṃ manotāpaṃ,

Ghare duccaritāni ca;

Vañcanañca [M 0.0007] avamānaṃ,

Paṇḍito na pakāsaye.

A wise person should not reveal loss of wealth, mental anguish, misconduct in the home, deception, or disgrace.

29.

29.

Pattānurūpakaṃ vākyaṃ,

Sabhāvarūpakaṃ piyaṃ;

Attānurūpakaṃ kodhaṃ,

Yo jānāti sa paṇḍito.

Speech that is appropriate to the occasion, pleasant and appropriate to the assembly, and anger that is appropriate to oneself—he who knows these is a wise person.

30.

30.

A-dhanassa rasaṃ khādā,

A-balassa hathā narā;

A-paññassa vākya-kathā,

Ummattaka-samā ime.

The eating of dainties by the poor, the use of force by the weak, and the discourse of the unwise—these are like unto a madman.

31.

31.

Anavhāyaṃ gamayanto,

A-pucchā bahu-bhāsako;

Atta-guṇaṃ pakāsento,

Ti-vidhaṃ hīna-lakkhaṇaṃ.

Going when uninvited, speaking much without being asked, and proclaiming one's own virtues—these are the three marks of an inferior person.

32.

32.

Appa-rūpo bahuṃ bhāso,

Appa-pañño pakāsito;

Appa-pūro ghaṭo khobhe,

Appa-khīrā gāvī cathe.

One of little form speaks much; one of little wisdom is thus revealed. A half-filled pot makes much noise; a cow with little milk lows loudly.

33.

33.

Maṇḍūkepi ukre sīhe,

Kākaggahe piye piye;

A-paṇḍīpi [M 0.0008] paṇḍī hutvā,

Dhīrā pucche vaye vaye.

Just as lions are not found among frogs, nor dear ones among crows, so even if an unwise person becomes wise, the discerning will question them repeatedly.

34.

34.

Maṇḍūkepi ukre sīhe,

Sūkarepi uhe dīpe;

Biḷāre sadise byagghe,

Sabba dhīre sippa-same.

Just as there are no lions among frogs, nor elephants among pigs, yet cats resemble tigers, so all the wise are equal in skill.

35.

35.

Na titti rājā dhanamhi,

Paṇḍitopi su-bhāsite;

Cakkhuṃpi piya-dassane,

Jale sāgaro na titti.

A king is not satisfied with wealth, nor a wise person with well-spoken words, nor the eye with pleasant sights, nor the ocean with water.

36.

36.

Rūpa-yobbana-sampannā,

Visātha-kutha-sambhavā;

Vijjā-hīnā na sobhanti,

Nigandhā iva kiṃsukā.

Those endowed with beauty and youth, born of a great family, if they are devoid of knowledge, do not shine, like scentless Kiṃsuka flowers.

37.

37.

Hīne putto rājāmacco,

Bāla-putto ca paṇḍito;

A-dhanassa dhanaṃ bahu,

Purisānaṃ na maññatha.

The son of a low-born person who becomes a king's minister, the foolish son of a wise person, and much wealth for one who was poor—one should not hold such men in contempt.

38.

38.

Yo sippa-lobhena,

Bahuṃ gaṇhāti taṃ sippaṃ;

Mūgova [M 0.0009] supinaṃ passaṃ,

Kathetumpi na ussahe.

He who, out of greed for skills, acquires many a craft, is like a mute person seeing a dream: he is not even able to speak of it.

39.

39.

Bhijjetuṃ kumbhakāro,

Sobhetuṃ kumbha ghaṭṭati;

Na khipituṃ apāyesu,

Sissānaṃ vuḍḍhi-kāraṇā.

The potter strikes the pot to make it beautiful, not to cast it into a woeful state; this is for the sake of the students' growth.

40.

40.

Taggarañca palāsena,

Yo naro upanayhati;

Pattāpi surabhi vāyanti,

Evaṃ dhīrūpasevanā.

If a person wraps tagara powder with palāsa leaves, even the leaves will emit a fragrance. Such is the result of associating with the wise.

Paṇḍitakaṇḍo niṭṭhito.

The Chapter on the Wise is concluded.

Sujanakaṇḍo

The Chapter on the Virtuous

41.

41.

Sabbhireva samāsetha,

Sabbhi kubbetha santhavaṃ;

Sataṃ saddhammamaññāya,

Seyyo hoti na pāpiyo.

Associate only with the virtuous; cultivate friendship with the virtuous. By understanding the true Dhamma of the good, one becomes better, not worse.

42.

42.

Caja dujjana saṃsaggaṃ,

Bhaja sādhu samāgamaṃ;

Kara puññamahorattiṃ,

Sara niccamaniccataṃ.

Abandon association with the wicked; cultivate the company of the good. Perform meritorious deeds day and night; constantly recollect impermanence.

43.

43.

Yathā [M 0.0010] udumbarapakkā,

Bahirattakamevaca;

Anto kimīhi sampuṇṇā,

Evaṃ dujjanahaddayā.

Just as the ripe udumbara fruit is red on the outside, but inside is full of worms, so too are the hearts of the wicked.

44.

44.

Yathāpi panasāpakkā,

Bahi kaṇḍakameva ca;

Anto amatasampannā,

Evaṃ sujanahadayā.

Just as the ripe jackfruit has a thorny exterior, but inside is filled with nectar, so too are the hearts of the virtuous.

45.

45.

Sukkhopi candanataru na jahāti gandhaṃ,

Nāgo gato naramukhe na jahāti līḷaṃ;

Yantāgato madhurasaṃ na jahāti ucchu,

Dukkhopi paṇḍitajano na jahāti dhammaṃ.

Even when dry, the sandalwood tree does not abandon its fragrance; an elephant, though old, does not abandon its graceful movements; sugarcane, when put through a press, does not abandon its sweetness; a wise person, though met with suffering, does not abandon the Dhamma.

46.

46.

Sīho nāma jighacchāpi,

Paṇṇādīni na khādati;

Sīho nāma kisocāpi,

Nāgamaṃsaṃ na khādati.

A lion, even when hungry, does not eat grass and the like; a lion, even when emaciated, does not eat the flesh of an elephant.

47.

47.

Kula-jāto kula-putto,

Kula-vaṃsa-surakkhato;

Attanā dukkha-pattopi,

Hīna-kammaṃ na kāraye.

A son of good family, born of noble lineage and of a well-protected line, even when afflicted by suffering, should not perform a base deed.

48.

48.

Candanaṃ [M 0.0011] sītalaṃ loke,

Tato candaṃva sītalaṃ;

Canda-candanasītamhā,

Sādhu vākyaṃ subhāsitaṃ.

Sandalwood is cool in the world, and the moon is cooler than that. Cooler than the moon and sandalwood is the well-spoken word of the virtuous.

49.

49.

Udeyya bhāṇu pacchime,

Merurājā nameyyapi;

Sītalā narakaggipi,

Pabbatagge ca uppalaṃ.

Vikase na viparītaṃ,

Sādhuvāyyaṃ kudācanaṃ.

The sun might rise in the west, Mount Meru might bow, the fire of hell might grow cool, and a lotus might bloom on a mountain peak; yet the word of the virtuous would never be otherwise.

50.

50.

Sukhā rukkhassa chāyāva,

Tato ñāti mātā pitu;

Tato ācariyo rañño,

Tato buddhassanekadhā.

Pleasant is the shade of a tree; more pleasant than that are one's relatives, mother, and father. More pleasant still are one's teacher and the king; and more pleasant than these, in manifold ways, is the Buddha.

51.

51.

Bhamarā pupphamicchanti,

Guṇamicchanti sujanā;

Makkhikā pūtimicchanti,

Dosamicchanti dujjanā.

Bees desire flowers; the virtuous desire good qualities. Flies desire filth; the wicked desire faults.

52.

52.

Mātāhīnassa dubbhāsā,

Pitāhīnassa dukriyā;

Ubho [M 0.0012] mātā pitā hīnā,

Dubbhasāca dukīriyā.

For one lacking a mother, there is bad speech; for one lacking a father, there are bad deeds. For one lacking both mother and father, there is both bad speech and bad deeds.

53.

53.

Mātā seṭṭhassa subhāsā,

Pitā seṭṭhassa sukriyā;

Ubho mātā pitā seṭṭhā,

Subhāsāca sukīriyā.

For one with an excellent mother, there is good speech; for one with an excellent father, there are good deeds. For one with an excellent mother and father, there is both good speech and good deeds.

54.

54.

Saṅgāme sūra-micchanti,

Mantīsu akūtūhalaṃ;

Piyañca annapānesu,

Atthe jāte ca paṇḍitaṃ.

In battle, one desires a hero; in counsel, one who is unperturbed; amidst food and drink, a dear friend; and when a need arises, a wise person.

55.

55.

Sunakho sunakhaṃ disvā,

Dantaṃ dasseti hiṃsituṃ;

Dujjano sujanaṃ disvā,

Rosayaṃ hiṃsamicchati.

A dog, upon seeing another dog, shows its teeth, intending to harm. A wicked person, upon seeing a virtuous person, becomes angry, wishing to harm.

56.

56.

Mā ca vegena kiccāni,

Karosi kārāpesi vā;

Sahasā kāritaṃ kammaṃ,

Mando pacchānutappati.

Do not perform tasks with haste, nor cause others to do so; for a deed done rashly is what the fool later regrets.

57.

57.

Kodhaṃ vihitvā na kadācinasoce,

Makkhappahānaṃ isayo avaṇṇayuṃ;

Sabbesa [M 0.0013] phārusa-vacaṃ khametha,

Etaṃ khantiṃ uttamamāhu santo.

Having abandoned anger, one never sorrows; the sages praised the abandoning of malice. Endure all harsh speech; this patience the good declare supreme.

58.

58.

Dukkho nivāso sambādhe,

Ṭhāne asucisaṅkate;

Tato arimhi appiye,

Tatopi akataññunā.

Living in a crowded place is suffering; in a place unclean and perilous; more so among an enemy and the unloved; and even more so with the ungrateful.

59.

59.

Ovādeyyānusāseyya,

Gāpakā ca nivāraye;

Satañhi so piyo hoti,

Asataṃ hoti appiyo.

One should advise and instruct, and restrain from wrongdoing; for such a one is dear to the good, and disliked by the bad.

60.

60.

Uttamattanivātena,

Sūraṃ bhedena nijjaye;

Nīcaṃ appaka dānena,

Vīriyena samaṃ jaye.

One should win over the superior with humility, the valiant by causing division, the lowly with a small gift, and an equal with energy.

61.

61.

Na visaṃ visamiccāhu,

Dhanaṃ saṅghassa uccate;

Visaṃ ekaṃva hanati,

Sabbaṃ saṅghassa santakaṃ.

They say poison is not the true poison; the wealth of the Sangha is called the poison. Poison kills but one person; the property of the Sangha destroys all.

62.

62.

Javane bhadraṃ jānanti,

Baliddañca vāhenā;

Duhena [M 0.0014] dhenuṃ jānanti,

Bhāsamānena paṇḍitaṃ.

By its speed, a noble steed is known; by its carrying, a beast of burden. By its milking, a cow is known; and by his speech, a wise man.

63.

63.

Dhanamappampi sādhūnaṃ,

Kūpe vāriva nissayo;

Bahuṃ api asādhūnaṃ,

Naca vārīva aṇṇave.

Even a little wealth of the virtuous is a resource, like water in a well; but much wealth of the wicked is not, like the water in the ocean.

64.

64.

Najjo pivanti no āpaṃ,

Rukkhā khādanti no phalaṃ;

Vassanti kvaci no meghā,

Paratthāya sataṃ dhanaṃ.

Rivers do not drink their own water; trees do not eat their own fruit; clouds do not rain for themselves. The wealth of the good is for the benefit of others.

65.

65.

Apattheyyaṃ na pattheyya,

A cinteyyaṃ na cintaye;

Dhammameva sucinteyya,

Kālaṃ moghaṃ na accaye.

Do not desire what should not be desired; do not think on what should not be thought. Think well on the Dhamma alone; let not time pass in vain.

66.

66.

Acintitampi bhavati,

Cintitampi vinassati;

Na hi cintāmayā bhogā,

Itthiyā purisassavā.

Even the unthought-of comes to be; even the thought-of is destroyed. For possessions are not born of thought, for a woman or for a man.

67.

67.

Asantassa piyo hoti,

Sante na kurute piyaṃ;

Asataṃ [M 0.0015] dhammaṃ roceti,

Taṃ parābhavato mukhaṃ.

He holds the wicked dear and does not befriend the virtuous; he approves of the teaching of the wicked—that is a cause of downfall.

Sujanakaṇḍo niṭṭhito.

The Chapter on the Good Person is finished.

4. Bāladujjanakaṇḍo

4. The Chapter on Fools and the Wicked

68.

68.

Atippiyo na kātabbo,

Khalo kotūhalaṃ karo;

Sirasā vahyamānopi,

Aḍḍhapūro ghaṭo yathā.

One should not be overly fond of a quarrelsome, meddlesome person; for even when carried on the head, he is like a half-filled pot.

69.

69.

Sappo duṭṭho khalo duṭṭho,

Sappo duṭṭhataro khalo;

Mantosadhehi so sappo,

Khalo kenupasammati.

A snake is wicked, and a rogue is wicked; but a rogue is more wicked than a snake. A snake is pacified by mantras and herbs, but by what is a rogue pacified?

70.

70.

Yo bālo maññati bālyaṃ,

Paṇḍito vāpi tena so;

Bālova paṇḍitamānī,

So ve bāloti vuccati.

The fool who knows his foolishness is wise at least to that extent; but the fool who thinks himself wise is called a fool indeed.

71.

71.

Madhuṃva maññatī bālo,

Yāva pāpaṃ na paccati;

Yadāca [M 0.0016] paccatī pāpaṃ,

Atha dukkhaṃ nigacchati.

So long as an evil deed has not ripened, the fool thinks it is like honey; but when the evil ripens, then the fool comes to suffering.

72.

72.

Na sādhu balavā bālo,

Sahasā vindate dhanaṃ;

Kāyassa bhedā duppañño,

Nirayaṃ sopapajjati.

It is not good when a strong fool gains wealth by force; at the breaking up of the body, the one of little wisdom is reborn in hell.

73.

73.

Ghare duṭṭho ca mūsīko ca,

Vane duṭṭho ca vānaro;

Sakuṇe ca duṭṭho kāko,

Nare duṭṭhoca brāhmaṇo.

In the house, the mouse is wicked; in the forest, the monkey is wicked; among birds, the crow is wicked; among men, the brahmin is wicked.

74.

74.

Dīghā jāgarato ratti,

Dīghaṃ santassa yojanaṃ;

Dīgho bālāna saṃsāro,

Saddhammaṃ a-vijānataṃ.

Long is the night to the wakeful; long is the league to the weary; long is saṃsāra for fools who do not know the good Dhamma.

75.

75.

Tila mattaṃ paresaṃva,

Appa dosañca passati;

Nāḷikerampi sadosaṃ,

Khala-jāto na passati.

He sees the faults of others, though they be as small as a sesame seed; but his own fault, though as large as a coconut, the base person does not see.

76.

76.

Nattadosaṃ pare jaññā,

Jaññā dosaṃ parassatu;

Guyhe [M 0.0017] kummo aṅgāni,

Para dosañca lakkhaye.

Let not others know one's own faults, but let one know the faults of others. As a tortoise hides its limbs, so should one conceal one's own and observe the faults of another.

77.

77.

Paṇḍitassa pasaṃsāya,

Daṇḍo bālena dīyate;

Paṇḍito paṇḍiteneva,

Vaṇṇitova suvaṇṇito.

For praising a wise person, a punishment is given by a fool; but a wise person praised by another wise person is truly well-praised.

78.

78.

Luddhaṃ atthena gaṇheyya,

Thaddhaṃ añjali kammunā;

Chandānuvattiyā mūḷhaṃ,

Yathābhūtena paṇḍitaṃ.

One should win over the greedy with a gift, the stubborn with a gesture of reverence, the foolish by compliance, and the wise with the truth.

Bāladujjanakaṇḍo niṭṭhito.

The Chapter on Fools and the Wicked is finished.

4. Mittakaṇḍo

4. The Chapter on Friends

79.

79.

Hitakāro paro bandhu,

Bandhūpi ahito paro;

A hito dehajo byādhi,

Hitaṃ araññamosadhaṃ.

A stranger who is helpful is a kinsman; a kinsman who is harmful is a stranger. A disease born in the body is harmful; a medicine from the forest is helpful.

80.

80.

Parokkhe guṇa-hantāraṃ,

Paccakkhe piyavādinaṃ;

Vajjeyya [M 0.0018] tādisaṃ mittaṃ,

Visakumbhe yathā madhuṃ.

One who speaks ill of you in your absence but speaks sweetly to your face—avoid such a friend, as one would a pot of poison with honey on its rim.

81.

81.

Dhanahīne caje mitto,

Puttadārā sahodarā;

Dhanavantaṃva sevanti,

Dhanaṃ loke mahā sakhā.

A friend deserts one who is without wealth, as do children, wife, and siblings; but they all attend upon the wealthy. Wealth is the greatest friend in the world.

82.

82.

Jāneyya pesena bhaccaṃ,

Bandhuṃ vāpi bhayāgate;

Appakāsu tathā mittaṃ,

Dārañca vibhavakkhaye.

One should know a servant by his task, a kinsman when danger comes, a friend in adversity, and a wife when wealth is destroyed.

83.

83.

So bandhu yo hite yutto,

Pitaro honti posako;

Taṃ mittaṃ yattha vissāso,

Sā bhariyā ca yassa nibbūti.

He is a kinsman who is devoted to one's welfare; parents are they who are nurturers; that is a friend in whom there is trust; and she is a wife who is one's peace.

84.

84.

Na vissase a-vissattaṃ,

Mittañcāpi na vissase;

Kadāci kupito mito,

Sabbaṃ dosaṃ pakāsaye.

Do not trust the untrustworthy, nor place excessive trust even in a friend; for a friend, when angered, may reveal all your faults.

85.

85.

Sakiṃ duṭṭhañca yo mittaṃ,

Puna sandhitumicchati;

So [M 0.0019] maccuṃ upagaṇhāti,

Gabbhamassatarī yathā.

He who desires to reconcile with a friend who was once malicious, embraces death, just as a mule with its womb.

86.

86.

Vahe a-mittaṃ khandhena,

Yāva kālo anāgato;

Tameva āgate kāle,

Sele bhinde ghaṭaṃ iva.

One should bear an enemy on one's shoulder, so long as the time has not yet come; but when that very time arrives, one should smash him like a pot on a rock.

87.

87.

Iṇaseso aggiseso,

Satruseso tatheva ca;

Punappunaṃ vivaḍḍhanti,

Tasmā sesaṃ na kāraye.

The remainder of a debt, the remainder of a fire, and the remainder of an enemy, just so; they grow again and again. Therefore, one should leave no remainder.

88.

88.

Padumaṃva mukhaṃ yassa,

Vācā candana sītalā;

Tādisaṃ nopaseveyya,

Hadayetu halāhalaṃ.

One whose face is like a lotus, whose speech is cool as sandalwood—one should not associate with such a one, in whose heart is deadly poison.

89.

89.

Na seve pharusaṃ sāmiṃ,

Naca seveyya macchariṃ;

Tato apaggaṇhaṃ sāmiṃ,

Neva niggahitaṃ tato.

Do not serve a harsh master, nor serve one who is miserly. Keep away from such a master, and you will not be oppressed by him.

90.

90.

Siṅgī paññāsa hatthena,

Vajje satena vājinaṃ;

Hatthiṃ [M 0.0020] dantiṃ sahassena,

Desa cāgena dujjanaṃ.

One should avoid a horned animal by fifty cubits, a horse by a hundred, a tusked elephant by a thousand, and a wicked person by abandoning the country.

91.

91.

Kudesañca kumittañca,

Kukulañca kubandhavaṃ;

Kudārañca kudāsañca,

Dūrato parivajjaye.

A bad country, a bad friend, a bad family, a bad kinsman, a bad wife, and a bad servant—one should avoid them from afar.

92.

92.

Rogāture ca dubbhikkhe,

Byasane sattu viggahe;

Rājadvāre susāne ca,

Ye tiṭṭhanti sumittakā.

In sickness and in famine, in misfortune and in conflict with an enemy, at the king's gate and in the charnel ground—they who stand by are true friends.

93.

93.

Sītavāco bahumitto,

Pharuso appamittako;

Upamaṃ ettha ñātabbā,

Canda-sūriya-rājūnaṃ.

One of gentle speech has many friends; a harsh person has few. An analogy here should be understood of the moon and the sun.

Mittakaṇḍo niṭṭhito.

The Chapter on Friends is concluded.

5. Itthikaṇḍo

5. The Chapter on Women

94.

94.

Kokilānaṃ saddaṃ rūpaṃ,

Nārīrūpaṃ patibbatā;

Vijjā [M 0.0021] rūpaṃ a-rūpānaṃ,

Khamā rūpaṃ tapassinaṃ.

For cuckoos, their voice is beauty; for a woman, devotion to her husband is beauty; for the unattractive, knowledge is beauty; for ascetics, patience is beauty.

95.

95.

Itthīnañca dhanaṃ rūpaṃ,

Purisānaṃ vijjā dhanaṃ;

Bhikkhūnañca dhanaṃ sīlaṃ,

Rājānañca dhanaṃ balaṃ.

For women, beauty is wealth; for men, knowledge is wealth; for bhikkhus, virtue is wealth; and for kings, power is wealth.

96.

96.

Tapassino kisā sobhā,

Thūlā sobhā catuppadā;

Purisā vijjavā sobhā,

Itthī sobhāsa sāmikā.

For an ascetic, leanness is beauty; for quadrupeds, stoutness is beauty; for men, possessing knowledge is beauty; for a woman, having a husband is her beauty.

97.

97.

Pañca ratyā sugandhabbā,

Satta ratyā dhanuggahā;

Eka māsā subhariyā,

Aḍḍha māsā sissā malā.

Fragrance is pleasant for five nights, an archer for seven nights, a good wife for one month, a bad disciple for half a month.

98.

98.

Hiṃ ramati paṅa,

Haṅa ramati poka.

Thī ramati pu,

Khu ramati dhaṃ.

A snake delights in an anthill, a swan delights in a lotus pond, a woman delights in her son, a wicked person delights in unrighteousness.

99.

99.

Jiṇṇamannaṃ pasaṃseyya,

Dārañca gatayobbanaṃ;

Raṇā punāgatā sūraṃ,

Sassañca gehamāgataṃ.

One should praise well-digested food, a wife whose youth has passed, a hero returned from battle, and the harvest brought into the home.

100.

100.

Dvatti-patikā [M 0.0022] nārī ca,

Bhikkhu dvatti-vihāriko;

Dvatti-pāsa-mutto pakkhī,

Kata-māyā bahūtharaṃ.

A woman with two husbands, a bhikkhu dwelling in two monasteries, a bird freed from two snares—their trickery is manifold.

101.

101.

Dujjanaṃ pahārādame,

Mittaṃ dame a-bhāṇikā;

Itthiñca byasanā dame,

Rāginaṃ appa bhojanā.

One should tame a wicked person with punishment, a friend by not speaking, a woman with misfortune, and a lustful person with little food.

102.

102.

Na ratti vinā candimā,

Vīciṃ vinā ca aṇṇavo;

Haṃsaṃ vinā pokkharaṇī,

Patiṃ kaññāca sobhate.

The night does not shine without the moon, nor the ocean without waves, nor the lotus pond without a swan; and a maiden does not shine without a husband.

103.

103.

Patinā janito bhogo,

Itthiyāva saṃgoppito;

Purisova hi padhāno,

Itthī suttaṃva sūciyā.

Wealth is generated by the husband and well-guarded by the wife; for the man is indeed the primary one, the woman is like the thread to the needle.

104.

104.

Sabbānadī vaṅkanadī,

Sabbe kaṭṭhamayā vanā;

Sabbitthiyo kare pāpaṃ,

Labhamāne nivātake.

All rivers are winding, all forests are made of wood; all women would do evil, when they find a secret place.

105.

105.

Vivādasīliṃ [M 0.0023] usūyabhāṇiniṃ,

Sampassataṇhiṃ bahupākabhuttiniṃ;

Aggantabhuttiṃ paragehavāsiniṃ,

Nāriṃ caje puttasatampi pūmā.

A woman who is quarrelsome, speaks with envy, is greedy, and a glutton; who eats before others and frequents others' homes—a man should forsake such a woman, even if she has a hundred sons.

106.

106.

Bhuttesu maṇḍesu janīva kantinī,

Guyheca ṭhāne bhaginīva hiriṇī;

Kammesu pattesu karoti dāsīva,

Bhayesu mantī sayanesu rāmaye;

Rūpīsu sikkhī kupanesu khantinī,

Sā nārī seṭṭhāti vadanti paṇḍitā;

Kāyassa bhedāca divebhaveyya sā.

To her dependents, she is like a loving mother; in private, modest like a sister; in her work, diligent like a servant; in danger, a counselor; in bed, a delight; in appearance, skilled; in anger, patient—such a woman is called the best by the wise; and at the breaking up of the body, she arises in a divine world.

107.

107.

Sāmā migakkhī tanumajjhagattā,

Sūrū sukesī samadantapantī;

Gambhīranābhī yuvatī susīlī,

Hīne kule jātāpi vivāhyā.

Of fine complexion, with eyes like a deer, a slender waist, beautiful thighs, lovely hair, and even rows of teeth; deep-navelled, youthful, and of good conduct—even if born in a humble family, she is worthy of marriage.

108.

108.

Saradaṃratu-kālānaṃ,

Bhariyānaṃ rūpavatī;

Jeṭṭho padhānaṃ puttānaṃ,

Disānaṃ uttarādisā.

Among the seasons, autumn is best; among wives, the beautiful one; among sons, the eldest is chief; among directions, the north.

109.

109.

Yā icche puriso hotuṃ,

Jāti jāti punappunaṃ;

Sāmikaṃ [M 0.0024] apacāyeyya,

Indaṃva pāricārikā.

She who wishes to be a man, in birth after birth, again and again, should honor her husband, like a handmaid serving Indra.

110.

110.

Yo icche puriso hotuṃ,

Jāti jāti punappunaṃ;

Paradānaṃ vivajjeyya,

Dhotapādova kaddamaṃ.

A man who wishes to be reborn again and again, from birth to birth, should avoid the property of others, as one with washed feet avoids mud.

111.

111.

Atikkanta vayo poso,

Āneti timbaruttaniṃ;

Tassā issā asaddhāti,

Taṃ parābhavato mukhaṃ.

A man past his prime who brings home a very young wife, and out of jealousy for her cannot sleep—this is a cause of his downfall.

Itthikaṇḍo niṭṭhito.

The Chapter on Women is concluded.

6. Rājakaṇḍo

6. The Section on Kings

112.

112.

Ekayāmaṃ saye rājā,

Dviyāmaññeva paṇḍito;

Gharāvāso tiyāmaṃva,

Catuyāmaṃ tu yācako.

A king sleeps for one watch; a wise man, for two; a householder, for three; but a beggar, for four.

113.

113.

Dhanavā sutavā rājā,

Nadī vejjo cimepañca;

Yattha [M 0.0025] dese na vijjanti,

Na tattha divasaṃ vase.

A wealthy man, a learned man, a king, a river, and a physician—these five; in a land where they are not found, one should not dwell even for a day.

114.

114.

Yasmiṃ padese na māno,

Na pemaṃ naca bandhavā;

Naca vijjāgamo koci,

Na tattha divasaṃ vase.

In a place where there is no honor, no affection, and no kinsmen, nor any acquisition of knowledge, one should not dwell there even for a day.

115.

115.

Aputtakaṃ gharaṃ suññaṃ,

Raṭṭhaṃ suññaṃ arājakaṃ;

A sippassa mukhaṃ suññaṃ,

Sabba suññaṃ daliddakā.

A childless home is empty; a kingdom without a king is empty; the mouth of one without a craft is empty; all is empty for the destitute.

116.

116.

Dhanamiccheyya vāṇijjo,

Vijjamicche bhajesutaṃ;

Puttamicche taruṇitthiṃ,

Rājāmaccaṃ vasaṃ game.

A merchant desires wealth; one devoted to learning desires knowledge; a young woman desires a son; a king desires to bring his ministers into submission.

117.

117.

Naṭṭhoyati asantuṭṭho,

Santuṭṭho ca mahīpati;

Lajjā ca gaṇikā naṭṭhā,

Nillajjā kuladhītikā.

The discontented one is ruined; the contented one is a king. A modest courtesan is ruined, and so is a shameless daughter of a good family.

118.

118.

Pakkhīnaṃ balamākāso,

Macchānamudakaṃ balaṃ;

Dubbalassa [M 0.0026] balaṃ rājā,

Kumārānaṃ rudaṃ balaṃ.

The sky is the strength of birds; water is the strength of fish; the king is the strength of the weak; weeping is the strength of children.

119.

119.

Khamā jāgariyuṭṭhānaṃ,

Saṃvibhāgo dayikkhaṇā;

Nāyakassa guṇā ete,

Icchitabbā sataṃ guṇā.

Patience, vigilance, exertion, sharing, compassion, and consideration—these are the virtues of a leader, virtues that ought to be desired by the good.

120.

120.

Sakiṃ vadanti rājāno,

Sakiṃ samaṇabrāhmaṇā;

Sakiṃ sappurisā loke,

Esa dhammo sanantano.

Kings speak but once; ascetics and brahmins speak but once; the good people in the world speak but once. This is the eternal law.

121.

121.

Alaso gihī kāmabhogī na sādhu,

Asaññato pabbajito na sādhu;

Rājā anisammakārī na sādhu,

Paṇḍito kodhano taṃpi na sādhu.

A lazy householder who indulges in sensual pleasures is not good; an unrestrained renunciant is not good; a king who acts without due consideration is not good; and a wise man prone to anger—that too is not good.

122.

122.

Bahavo yattha nettāro,

Sabbe paṇḍitamānino;

Sabbe mahattamicchanti,

Tesaṃ kammaṃ vinassati.

Where there are many leaders, all esteeming themselves as wise, all desiring greatness—their undertaking is ruined.

123.

123.

Āyaṃ khayaṃ sayaṃ jaññā,

Rājā sayaṃ katākataṃ;

Niggahe [M 0.0027] niggahetabbaṃ,

Paggahe paggahārahaṃ.

A king should know for himself income and expenditure, and for himself what has been done and what has not been done. He should restrain what ought to be restrained, and support what is worthy of support.

124.

124.

Piṭṭhitokkaṃ niseveyya,

Kucchināva hutāsanaṃ;

Sāmikaṃ sabbabhāgena,

Paralokaṃ amohavā.

One should approach a fire with one's front, but a master from behind. One should serve one's lord in every respect, undeluded about the world to come.

125.

125.

Aggi āpo itthimūḷho,

Sappo rāja-kulānica;

Apayantena gantabbā,

Acceka-pāṇahārakā.

Fire, water, a foolish woman, a snake, and royal families—these should be approached with caution, for they are sudden takers of life.

126.

126.

Paduṭṭha-bhariya-saṃvāso,

Paduṭṭha citta dāsako;

Sa-sappe ca ghare vāso,

Maccu eva na saṃsayo.

Living with a corrupt wife, with a wicked-minded servant, or in a house with a snake—this is surely death, there is no doubt.

127.

127.

Mūḷha sisso padesena,

Kunārī bharaṇena ca;

Asatā sampayogena,

Paṇḍitoppavasīdati.

By instructing a foolish pupil, by supporting a wicked woman, and through association with the ignoble, even a wise man comes to ruin.

128.

128.

Mātā puttakaraṃ pāpaṃ,

Sissapāpaṃ gurukatā;

Rājā [M 0.0028] raṭṭhakaraṃ pāpaṃ,

Rājapāpaṃ purohito.

The mother bears the sin done by her child; the teacher, the sin of his pupil; the king, the sin done by his kingdom; and the royal chaplain, the sin of the king.

129.

129.

Akodhena jine kodhaṃ,

Asādhuṃ sādhunā jine;

Jine macchariṃ dānena,

Saccenālīkavādinaṃ;

Conquer anger with non-anger; conquer evil with good; conquer miserliness with giving; and the liar with truth.

130.

130.

Adantaṃ damanaṃ dānaṃ,

Dānaṃ sabbattha sādhakaṃ;

Dānena piya vācāya,

Unnamanti namanti ca;

Giving is the taming of the untamed; giving is universally effective. Through giving and with pleasant speech, people are both exalted and pay homage.

131.

131.

Dānaṃ sinehabhesajjaṃ,

Maccheraṃ dosanosadhaṃ;

Dānaṃ yasassī bhesajjaṃ,

Maccheraṃ kapaṇosadhaṃ.

Giving is the medicine that creates affection; miserliness is the medicine that creates aversion. Giving is the medicine that brings fame; miserliness is the medicine that brings poverty.

132.

132.

Bahūnamappasārānaṃ,

Sāmaggiyā jayaṃ jaye;

Tiṇehi vattate yottaṃ,

Tena nāgopi bajjhate.

Through the unity of many who are weak, victory may be won. A rope is twisted from blades of grass; with it, even an elephant is bound.

133.

133.

Sahāyo asamatthopi,

Tejasā kiṃkarissati;

Nivāte [M 0.0029] jalito aggi,

Sayame vūpasampati.

Even if spirited, what can a powerless ally accomplish? A fire, blazing in a place without wind, is extinguished by itself.

134.

134.

Na raññā samakaṃ bhuñje,

Kāmabhogaṃ kudācanaṃ;

Ākappaṃ rasa bhuttiṃvā,

Mālā gandha vilepanaṃ;

Vatthaṃ sabbaalaṅkāraṃ,

Na raññā sadisaṃ kare.

One should never enjoy sensual pleasures equally with the king. Attire, the enjoyment of fine flavors, garlands, perfumes, ointments, clothing, and all adornments—one should not make these like the king's.

135.

135.

Na me rājā sakhā hoti,

Na rājā hoti methuno;

Eso sāmiko mayhanti,

Citte niṭṭhaṃ suthāpaye.

The king is not my friend, nor is the king my companion. 'He is my lord'—this conclusion one should firmly establish in the mind.

136.

136.

Nātidūre bhaje rañño,

Nāccāsannopavātake;

Ujuke nātininne ca,

Na bhaje uccamāsane.

Chadose vajje sevako,

Tiṭṭhe aggiṃva saṃyato.

One should not attend the king from too far, nor too near, nor downwind. Not directly in front, nor too low, nor should one occupy a high seat. A servant should avoid these six faults and remain restrained, as if before a fire.

137.

137.

Guṇī sabbaññu tulyopi,

Nasobhati anissayo;

Anagghamopi [M 0.0030] maṇiseṭṭho,

Hemaṃ nissāya sobhati.

Even a virtuous person, equal to the Omniscient One, does not shine without a patron. Even the finest priceless jewel shines only when it is set in gold.

Rājakaṇḍo niṭṭhito.

The Section on Kings is concluded.

7. Pakiṇṇakakaṇḍo

7. The Miscellaneous Section

138.

138.

Itthimisse kutosīlaṃ,

Maṃsa bhakkhe kutodayā;

Surā pāne kutosaccaṃ,

Mahālobhe kutohirī;

Mahātande kutosippaṃ,

Mahā kodhe kutodhanaṃ.

In one devoted to women, whence virtue? In a meat-eater, whence compassion? In a drinker of intoxicants, whence truthfulness? In one of great greed, whence shame? In one of great laziness, whence skill? In one of great anger, whence wealth?

139.

139.

Surā yogo vikālo ca,

Samajja caraṇālasaṃ;

Khiḍḍādhutto pāpamitto,

Bhoganāsamukhā ime.

Addiction to intoxicants, frequenting festivals at untimely hours, idleness, indulgence in gambling, and association with evil friends—these are the causes of the ruin of one's wealth.

140.

140.

Divā nādikkhā vattabbaṃ,

Ratto nāvacanena ca;

Sañcareyya bhayā bhīto,

Vane vanacarī yathā.

By day one should not go about unseen, nor at night be without a message. One should wander fearful and afraid, like a forest-dweller in the forest.

141.

141.

Jīvantāpi matāpañca,

Byāsena parikittitā;

Dukkhito [M 0.0031] byādhitomūḷho,

Iṇavā nityasevako.

Five are declared by Vyāsa to be dead even while living: the one in distress, the sick, the deluded, the debtor, and the constant servant.

142.

142.

Anāgataṃ bhayaṃ disvā,

Dūrato parivajjaye;

Āgatañca bhayaṃ disvā,

A bhīto hoti paṇḍito.

Seeing a future danger, one should avoid it from afar. And seeing a danger that has arrived, the wise one is unafraid.

143.

143.

Niddāluko pamattoca,

Sukhatto rogavālaso;

Mahiccho kammārāmoca,

Satte te satthavajjitā.

The sleepy, the heedless, one fond of comfort, the diseased, the lazy, one of great ambition, and one delighting in activity—these seven are rejected by the Teacher.

144.

144.

Duggataṃ gaccha he lābha,

Lābhī lābhena pūrati;

Thale pavassa pajjunna,

Sindhu āpena pūrati;

Natthidaṃ kammappadhānakaṃ.

O gain, go to the unfortunate! But the gainer is filled with gain. O rain cloud, rain upon the dry land! But the ocean is filled with water. In this, past action is not the chief cause.

145.

145.

Na hi koci kate kicce,

Kattāraṃ samupekkhate;

Tasmā sabbāni kiccāni,

Sāva sesena kāraye.

Indeed, no one disregards the doer while a task is yet to be completed. Therefore, one should have all tasks performed with something remaining.

146.

146.

Tūlaṃ sallahukaṃ loke,

Tato cāpalla-jātiko;

Tato [M 0.0032] vuḍḍha manovādo,

Pamatto buddhasāsane.

Cotton is very light in the world; lighter than that is a frivolous person; lighter than that is the counsel of an elder; and lighter still is one who is heedless in the Buddha's teaching.

147.

147.

Pāsāṇachattaṃ garukaṃ,

Tato devānacikkhaṇaṃ;

Tato vuḍḍhānamovādo,

Tato buddhassa sāsanaṃ.

A stone umbrella is heavy; heavier than that is a proclamation of the devas; heavier than that is the advice of elders; and heavier still is the teaching of the Buddha.

148.

148.

Kāyassa dakkhiṇa hattho,

Doso ettha kaniṭṭhako;

Kaṇṇa ghānāna-makkhīnaṃ,

Vāmo tu pāda-pāsako.

The right hand of the body; here anger is the smallest fault. Of the ears, nose, and eyes; the left foot, however, is a snare.

149.

Tambūlassa majjha patte,

Kuvero rakkhatī sadā;

Mūlamhi rakkhati yakkho,

Aggamhi kālakaṇṇikā;

Tāni bhuñjeyya chinditvā,

Sirī evaṃ pavaḍḍhati.

In the middle of the betel leaf, Kuvera always protects; at the root, a yakkha protects; at the tip, Kālakaṇṇikā. Having cut off those parts, one should consume it; thus does prosperity increase.

150.

150.

Sampuṇṇarakkho brahmāva,

Accurakkho ca bissaṇo;

Tasmā hi te pūjayantu,

Sadā mānenti taṃ naraṃ.

Brahmā is completely protected, and Viṣṇu is exceedingly protected. Therefore, let people worship them; they always esteem that person.

151.

151.

Goṇā [M 0.0033] hi sabbagihīnaṃ,

Posakā bhogadāyakā;

Tasmā hi mātā pitūva,

Mānaye sakkareyya ca.

Cattle are indeed the nourishers of all householders, providers and givers of enjoyment. Therefore, one should honor and revere them as one's mother and father.

152.

152.

Yeca khādanti gomaṃsaṃ,

Mātu maṃsaṃva khādare;

Matesu tesu gijjhānaṃ,

Dade sote ca vāhaye.

Those who eat the flesh of cattle, it is as if they eat their own mother's flesh. When they are dead, their ears should be given to the vultures, and they should be cast away.

153.

153.

Gurusiddho sippārambho,

Ravi sokrā ca majjhimo;

Na sippo buddhacandaro,

Sorī aṅgāca maraṇaṃ.

For beginning a craft, Thursday brings success. Sunday and Friday are middling. No craft should be started on Wednesday or Monday. Saturday and Tuesday bring death.

154.

154.

Aṭṭhamiyaṃ guruṃ hanti,

Sissaṃ hanti catuddasiṃ;

Sippaṃ hanti dasa sippaṃ,

Mātāpitā ca puṇṇamiṃ.

On the eighth day, it harms the teacher; on the fourteenth, it harms the pupil; on the tenth, it harms the craft; and on the full moon, it harms mother and father.

155.

155.

Nāḷikaṃ satta nabhuñje,

Na lābuṃ navamaṃ tathā;

Dvādasa prinnaṃtriminaṃ,

Bhuñje sippaṃ vinassati.

One should not eat nāḷika on the seventh day, nor likewise a gourd on the ninth. If one eats these on the twelfth, thirteenth, or thirtieth day, one's craft is destroyed.

156.

156.

Ekaṃ [M 0.0034] caje kulaatthaṃ,

Gāmassatthaṃ kuvaṃ caje;

Gāma caje janapadatthaṃ,

Attatthaṃ pathaviṃ caje.

For the sake of the family, one should sacrifice an individual. For the sake of the village, one should sacrifice a family. For the sake of the country, one should sacrifice a village. For the sake of the self, one should sacrifice the world.

157.

157.

Desaṃ ossajja gacchanti,

Sīho sappuriso gajo;

Tattheva nidhanaṃ yanti,

Kāko kāpuriso migo.

Abandoning their own place, they go forth: the lion, the good person, the elephant. But in that very place they meet their end: the crow, the vile person, the deer.

158.

158.

Yamhi padese na māno,

Na pemaṃ na ca bandhavā;

Na ca vijjāgāho koci,

Na tattha vasanaṃ kare.

In a place where there is no respect, no affection, and no kinsmen, and where there is no acquisition of knowledge, one should not make one's home.

159.

159.

Caratyekena pādena,

Tiṭṭhatyekena paṇḍito;

A nisamma paraṃ ṭhānaṃ,

Na pubbamālayaṃ jahe.

The wise person travels with one foot and stands with the other; without having fully surveyed the next location, one should not abandon the former abode.

160.

160.

Dhana dhañña payogesu,

Tathā vijjāgamesu ca;

Dūtesu apacāresu,

Cajjā lajjā tadā bhave.

In dealings with wealth and grain, and likewise in the acquisition of knowledge, in matters of messengers and conduct, one should then abandon shyness.

161.

161.

Dvi [M 0.0035] guṇo thīnamāhāro,

Buddhicāpi catugguṇo;

Chagguṇo hoti vāyāmo,

Kāmotvaṭṭha-guṇo bhave.

A woman's appetite is twofold, her intelligence fourfold, her effort sixfold, and her sexual desire is eightfold.

162.

162.

Pabbe pabbe kamenucchu,

Visesarasavāggato;

Tathā sumettiko sādhu,

Viparītova dujjano.

Joint by joint, in sequence, the sugarcane attains the highest, most excellent flavor. Likewise is a good person of friendly disposition; the wicked person is the very opposite.

163.

163.

Kassako vāṇijo macco,

Samaṇo suta sīlavā;

Tesu vipula jātesu,

Raṭṭhampi vipulaṃ siyā.

The farmer, the merchant, the citizen, the contemplative, the learned and virtuous one—when these become prosperous, the kingdom too shall be prosperous.

164.

164.

Asajjhāya malā mantā,

Anuṭṭhāna malā gharā;

Malaṃ vaṇṇassa kosajjaṃ,

Pamādo rakkhato malaṃ.

Non-recitation is the stain of sacred verses; non-exertion is the stain of a household; laziness is the stain of beauty; heedlessness is the stain of a guardian.

165.

165.

Hīnānaṃ gacchate vittaṃ,

Vīrānaṃ santakattanaṃ;

Vadanti ca hīnā janā,

Pubba-kammappadhānakā.

Wealth goes to the inferior, while the possessions of the brave are diminished. And the inferior people say that past karma is the primary cause.

166.

166.

Na [M 0.0036] vadanti cevaṃdhīrā,

Vāyamiṃsu sabbakamme;

Na ce sijjhati taṃ kammaṃ,

A-phalaṃ eva ko doso.

The wise do not speak thus; they strive in every deed. If that deed does not succeed, what fault is there in its being fruitless?

167.

167.

Nīcaṃ kulaṃ nipaññaṃ vā,

Nirūpaṃ nibalaṃ samaṃ;

Imaṃ kālaṃ chuttakālaṃ,

Dhanameva visesakaṃ.

A family may be lowly or lacking in wisdom, without beauty, without strength, and ordinary; in this time of scarcity, wealth alone is what distinguishes.

Pakiṇṇakakaṇḍo niṭṭhito.

The Section on Miscellanea is concluded.

Paṇḍito sujano kaṇḍo,

Dujjano mitta-itthi ca;

Rājā pakiṇṇako cāti,

Satta-kaṇḍa-vibhūsitaṃ.

The Wise, the Virtuous, the Wicked, Friends, Women, the King, and the Miscellanea—thus is this work adorned with seven sections.

Visuddhā cāra-therena,

Visuddhārāma-vāsinā;

Sabba-kulānamatthāya,

Visodhitaṃ pathakkhaye.

Revised by the Elder Visuddhācāra, a resident of Visuddhārāma, for the benefit of all families, at the end of the path.
