Namo tassa bhagavato arahato sammāsambuddhassa

Homage to the Blessed One, the Arahant, the Perfectly Self-Enlightened One.

Mahārahanīti

The Great Treatise on Proper Conduct

Paṇḍitakathā

The Discourse on the Wise

1.

1.

Mahāraharahaṃ [M 0.0001] sakya,

Muniṃ nīvaraṇā taṇhā;

Muttaṃ muttaṃ sudassanaṃ,

Vande bodhivaraṃ varaṃ.

I venerate the Great Arahant, the Sakyan Sage, freed from hindrances and craving, the utterly liberated one, beautiful to behold; I venerate the excellent, supreme Enlightenment.

2.

2.

Muninā [M 0.0002] munināgena,

Duṭṭhapabbajjitā jitā;

Yathā yathāasaddhamma-

Puraṇā puraṇādayo.

By the Sage, the chief of sages, wicked renunciants were conquered, just as the false Dhamma, ancient corruptions, and the like were overcome.

3.

3.

Tathā tathāgatovādā,

Nugate lokanītiyaṃ;

Katayogena vidūnā,

Jeyyovānattha kārino.

Thus, when the Tathagata's teachings are followed in worldly conduct, those who cause harm are to be conquered by the wise through accomplished effort.

4.

4.

Nitīdha [M 0.0003] jantunaṃ sāro,

Mittācariyāca pītarā;

Nītimā subuddhi byatto,

Sutavā atthadassimā.

Here, the essence for beings is proper conduct, friends, teachers, and fathers. Such a one is principled, wise, skilled, learned, and discerning of the goal.

5.

5.

Kaviheraññakā katvā,

Suttattaṃ satthakañcanaṃ;

Gajjapajjādyālaṅkāraṃ,

Karontīdha manorammaṃ.

Poet-goldsmiths, having fashioned well-composed golden treatises, here create a delightful adornment of prose, verse, and so on.

6.

6.

Vutyaṃ [M 0.0004] visadañāṇassa,

Ñāto atthotarassa na;

Surappabhāya ādāso,

Chāyaṃ disse na mākare.

Clear knowledge of conduct is known by one of higher purpose, not by the lesser; just as by divine radiance a reflection is seen in a mirror, but not its source.

7.

7.

Mahātejopi tejoyaṃ,

Mattikaṃ namudukaṃkaro;

Āpo āpeti mudukaṃ,

Sādhuvācāca kakkhaḷaṃ.

Even great heat does not make clay soft; water brings softness, and good speech softens the harsh.

8.

8.

Mudunāva [M 0.0005] ripuṃ jeti,

Mudunā jeti dāruṇaṃ;

Nosiddhaṃ mudunā kiñci,

Yatvato mudunā jaye.

By gentleness one conquers the foe, by gentleness one conquers the cruel; nothing whatsoever is unachieved by gentleness, therefore one should conquer with gentleness.

9.

9.

Candanaṃ sītalaṃ loke,

Tato candaṃva sītalaṃ;

Candanacandasītamhā,

Vākyaṃ sādhusubhāsitaṃ.

Sandalwood is cool in the world, cooler than that is the moon; cooler than sandalwood and the moon is the well-spoken word of the virtuous.

10.

10.

Pattakalloditaṃ [M 0.0006] appaṃ,

Vākyaṃ subhāsitaṃ bhave;

Khudditassa kadannaṃpi,

Suttaṃ sāduraso siyā.

A little word spoken at the opportune time may become a well-spoken word; for the hungry, even coarse food is a delight, and sleep may be a sweet savor.

11.

11.

Satthakāpi bahūvācā,

Nādarā bahubhāṇino;

Sopakāramudāsīnaṃ,

Nanudiṭṭhaṃ nadījalaṃ.

Though teachers may speak at length, the garrulous are not respected. To one who is indifferent, help is like river water that flows by unseen.

12.

12.

Saṃsāravisarukkhassa[M 0.0007],

Dvayamevāmataṃphalaṃ;

Subhāsitassasārādo,

Sādhūhirasamāgamo.

Of the poison-tree of saṃsāra, there are indeed two deathless fruits: savoring the essence of well-spoken words, and association with the good.

13.

13.

Pāsāṇachattaṃ garukaṃ,

Tato devānacikkhanā;

Tato vuḍḍhānamovādo,

Tato buddhassa sāsanaṃ;

A stone umbrella is heavy; heavier than that is the instruction of the gods; heavier than that is the advice of elders; heavier than that is the teaching of the Buddha.

14.

14.

Tulaṃ [M 0.0008] sallahukaṃ loke,

Tato capalajātiko;

Tatonovādiko tato,

Yati dhammepamādako.

In the world, cotton is very light; lighter than that is the fickle-minded one; lighter than that is the unteachable one; lighter than that is the monk heedless of the Dhamma.

15.

15.

Aha gacchati hāyantī,

Sattānamidha jīvitaṃ;

Tasmāhi māpamattatvaṃ,

Gacchantu jinasāsane.

Alas, the life of beings here goes on, diminishing; therefore, may they not become heedless in the Conqueror's teaching.

16.

16.

Paṇḍitassa pasaṃsāya,

Daṇḍobālena dīyate;

Paṇḍito paṇḍiteneva,

Vaṇṇitova suvaṇṇito.

A fool gives punishment in response to praise for a wise person; but a wise person praised by the wise is truly well-praised, like fine gold.

17.

17.

Satesu [M 0.0009] jāyate sūro,

Sahassesuca paṇḍito;

Vuttā satasahassesu,

Dātā bhavativā navā.

Among a hundred, a hero is born; among a thousand, a wise one; among a hundred thousand, a speaker; but whether a giver is to be found or not is uncertain.

18.

18.

Bhūpattaṃca paṇḍitattañca,

Nevatulyaṃ kadācipi;

Sadesepūjito rājā,

Buddho sabbatthapūjito.

Kingship and wisdom are never equal; a king is honored in his own land, the Buddha is honored everywhere.

19.

19.

Kvātibhāro [M 0.0010] samatthānaṃ,

Kiṃdūro byavahārinaṃ;

Kovideso savijjānaṃ,

Koparo piyavādinaṃ.

What is an excessive burden for the capable? What is a great distance for those engaged in affairs? What is a foreign land for the knowledgeable? Who is a stranger to the pleasant-speaking one?

20.

20.

Nanu te yevasantā no,

Sāgarā nakulācalā;

Appampi mariyādaṃ ye,

Saṃvaṭṭevi jahantino.

Surely, are not the good like oceans and great mountains? They are those who do not abandon their principles, even a little, even at the dissolution of the world-cycle.

21.

21.

Palayetītamariyādā[M 0.0011],

Bhavantikira sāgarā;

Mariyādātikaṃ sādhu,

Naicchanti palayepi.

Oceans, it is said, may transgress their boundaries at the time of the world's destruction; but the good do not desire to transgress their principles, even in destruction.

22.

22.

Sataṃ dīghāyukaṃ sabba,

Sattānaṃ sukhakāraṇaṃ;

Asataṃpana sabbesaṃ,

Dukkhahetu nasaṃsayo.

The good are the cause of long life and happiness for all beings; but the wicked are the cause of suffering for all, of this there is no doubt.

23.

23.

Pāmokkhe [M 0.0012] sujane sante,

Sabbepi sujanā janā;

Jātekasmiṃsāragandhe,

Sabbe gandhamayā dumā.

When a foremost good person is present, all people become good; when an essential fragrance arises in one tree, all trees become fragrant.

24.

24.

Attanā yadi ekena,

Vinatena mahājanā;

Vinayaṃ yanti sabbepi,

Ko taṃ nāseyya paṇḍito.

If, by a single humble and disciplined person, a great multitude all attain discipline, what wise person would destroy him?

25.

25.

Sarīrassa [M 0.0013] guṇānañca,

Dūramaccanta mantaraṃ;

Sarīraṃ khaṇaviddhaṃsi,

Kappantiṭṭhāyino guṇā.

Between the body and virtues, the difference is immense; the body is destroyed in a moment, while virtues endure for an aeon.

26.

26.

Yadi niccamaniccena,

Nimalaṃ malavāhinā;

Yo so kāyena labhetha,

Kiṃnaladdhaṃ bhave nukiṃ.

If, by means of this impermanent and defiled body, one could obtain that which is permanent and stainless, what then could not be gained?

27.

27.

Dhammatthakāmamokkhānaṃ,

Pāṇaṃ saṃsiddhikāraṇaṃ;

Tannighātā kinnahataṃ,

Rakkhanā kinnarakkhitaṃ.

Life is the cause for accomplishing Dhamma, wealth, desire, and liberation. By destroying it, what is not destroyed? By protecting it, what is not protected?

28.

28.

Attā [M 0.0014] bandhu manussassa,

Ripu attāva jantunaṃ;

Attāva niyato ñāti,

Attāva niyato ripu.

The self is a kinsman to humankind, the self indeed is a foe to beings; the self indeed is a certain kinsman, the self indeed is a certain foe.

29.

29.

Attanoparicāgena,

Yaṃsitamanurakkhanaṃ;

Karonti sajjanāyeva,

Na taṃ nītimatā mataṃ.

That the good protect what is clung to by sacrificing themselves—this is not esteemed by the principled.

30.

30.

Tiṇāni [M 0.0015] bhūmirudakaṃ,

Catutthī vākyasuṭṭhutā;

Etāni hi sataṃgehe,

Nochindante kadācipi.

Grass, earth, and water, and fourth, fitting speech; these indeed are never lacking in the home of the virtuous.

31.

31.

Ambuṃ pivantino najjo,

Rukkho khādatino phalaṃ;

Megho kvacipi no sassaṃ,

Paratthāya sataṃdhanaṃ.

Rivers do not drink their water; a tree does not eat its fruit; a cloud nowhere consumes the harvest. The wealth of the good is for the benefit of others.

32.

32.

Sataṃ pharusavācāhi,

Nayāti vikatiṃ mano;

Tiṇukkāhi nasakkāva,

Tāpetuṃ sāgare jalaṃ.

By harsh words, the mind of the good does not undergo alteration; indeed, it is not possible with grass-torches to heat the water in the ocean.

33.

33.

Sampatyaṃ [M 0.0016] mahattaṃceto,

Bhavatyuppalakomalaṃ;

Vippatyañca mahāsele,

Silāsaṅghādakakkaso.

In prosperity, the great mind becomes as tender as a water lily; and in adversity, as hard as a solid block of stone on a great mountain.

34.

34.

Atthaṃ mahantamāpajja,

Vijjaṃ sampattimevaca;

Careyyāmānathaddhoyo,

Paṇḍito so pavuccati.

Whoever, having attained great benefit and the accomplishment of knowledge, conducts himself without pride and stubbornness, he is called a wise man.

35.

35.

Nālabbhamabhipatthenti[M 0.0017],

Naṭṭhampi nacasocare;

Vippatyañca namuyhanti,

Yenarā teva paṇḍito.

They do not long for the unattainable, nor do they grieve for what is lost; and in adversity they are not bewildered—such people are indeed wise.

36.

36.

Gaṇṭhiṭṭhāne ekapade,

Nātimaññeyya paṇḍito;

Kiṃ akko veḷupabbhāro,

Timahādīpabhānudo.

A wise person should not disregard a difficult point in a single phrase. What is the sun, a load of bamboo? It is the illuminator of the three great continents.

37.

37.

Guṇadosesumekena,

Natthi koci vivajjito;

Sukhumālassa padumassa,

Naḷā bhavati kakkasā.

No one is entirely without virtues or faults; even the delicate lotus has a rough stalk.

38.

38.

Sumahantāni [M 0.0018] satthāni,

Dhārayanto bahussutā;

Chettāro saṃsayānantu,

Kilante lobhamohatā.

The very learned, holding the great scriptures and being dispellers of doubts, are nonetheless afflicted by greed and delusion.

39.

39.

Dosampi saguṇe disvā,

Guṇavādī vadantino;

Na loko vijjamānampi,

Cande passati lañchanaṃ.

Even having seen a fault in a virtuous person, those who praise virtue do not speak of it; just as the world does not see the blemish that exists on the moon.

40.

40.

Nipuṇe [M 0.0019] sutameseyya,

Vicinitvā sutatthiko;

Bhattaṃ hukkhaliyaṃ pakkaṃ,

Bhājanaṃpi tathā bhave.

A seeker of what is taught should investigate and seek out that which is subtle. As cooked rice in a flawed pot is ruined, so too would be the vessel.

41.

41.

Guṇā kubbanti dūtattaṃ,

Dūrepi vasataṃ sataṃ;

Ketake gandha ghāsāya,

Gacchanti bhamarā sayaṃ.

Virtues act as messengers for the good, even for those dwelling afar; bees go by themselves to the ketaka flower to partake of its fragrance.

42.

42.

Paṇḍito sutasampanno,

Yatthaatthīti cesuto;

Mahussāhena taṃṭhānaṃ,

Gantabbaṃ sutaresinā.

If it is heard, 'There is a wise person endowed with learning,' that place should be gone to with great effort by one who seeks learning.

43.

43.

Sabbasutaṃ [M 0.0020] madhīyetha,

Hīnamukkaṭṭha majjhimaṃ;

Sabbassaatthaṃ jāneyya,

Naca sabbaṃ payojaye;

Hoti tādisakokālo,

Yattha atthāvahaṃ sutaṃ.

One should study all that is heard—low, high, and middling. One should know the meaning of all, but not apply it all. For a time comes when what is learned brings benefit.

44.

44.

Ṭhito cirattanaṃ nīre,

Sakkharo nantaraṃsuto;

Dabbho nīrantike jāto,

Sabbakālantaraṃsuto.

A pebble, though remaining long in water, is not softened within; kusa grass, born near water, is always softened within.

45.

45.

Vasuṃ [M 0.0021] gaṇhanti dūraṭṭhā,

Pabbate ratanocite;

Na milakkhā samīpaṭṭhā,

Evaṃ bālā bahussute.

Though distant, men take treasure from a jewel-rich mountain, but the barbarians standing near do not. Thus it is with fools in the presence of the very learned.

46.

46.

Hiraññena migānaṃva,

Susīlaṃpi asīlino;

Adhammikassa dhammena,

Bālānaṃpi sutena kiṃ.

What use is gold to a deer, or good conduct to the immoral, or the Dhamma to the unrighteous, or learning to fools?

47.

47.

Sutena [M 0.0022] hadayaṭṭhena,

Khalo nevasusīlavā;

Madhunā koṭaraṭṭhena,

Nimbo kiṃ madhurāyate.

A wicked person with learning in his heart is not thereby virtuous; does a neem tree become sweet from honey placed in its hollow?

48.

48.

Sakiṃpi viññu dhīrena,

Karoti sahasaṅgamaṃ;

Attatthañca paratthañca,

Nibbānantaṃ sukhaṃ labhe.

If an intelligent person associates with the wise even once, he obtains his own welfare, the welfare of others, and the happiness that culminates in Nibbāna.

49.

49.

Haṃso majjhe nakākānaṃ,

Sīho gunnaṃ nasobhati;

Gadrabhānaṃ turaṅgono,

Bālānañca napaṇḍito.

A swan does not shine in the midst of crows, a lion does not shine among cattle, a horse does not shine among donkeys, nor a wise person among fools.

50.

50.

Ākiṇṇopi [M 0.0023] asabbhīva,

Asaṃsaṭṭhova bhaddako;

Bahunā sannajātena,

Gacche nummattakenidha.

Though surrounded by the wicked, the good person remains as if unassociated. Through much familiarity, one might indeed go to madness here.

51.

51.

Nadītire khatekūpe,

Araṇītālavaṇṭake;

Navade āpādi natthīti,

Mukheca vacanaṃ tathā.

As with a well dug on a riverbank, or a fire-drill with a palmyra stalk, one should not say, 'It has come to be, yet it is not.' Such is a mere word in the mouth.

52.

52.

Sutasanniccayā [M 0.0024] dhīrā,

Tuṇhibhūtā apucchitā;

Puṇṇā subhāsitenāpi,

Ghaṇḍādyaghaṭitāyathā.

The wise, having a store of learning, are silent when unasked; though full of well-spoken words, they are like an unstruck bell.

53.

53.

Apuṭṭho paṇḍito bherī,

Pajjunno hoti pucchito;

Bālo puṭṭho apuṭṭhoca,

Bahuṃ vikattate sadā.

Unasked, the wise man is like a drum; asked, he is like a thundercloud. A fool, whether asked or unasked, always boasts excessively.

54.

54.

Sampannaguṇā laṅkāro,

Sabbasatta hitāvaho;

Nacareyya parattatthaṃ,

Kuto so paṇḍito bhave.

If one who is an ornament of accomplished virtues and a bringer of welfare to all beings does not act for the good of others, how could he be a wise man?

55.

55.

Saparatthaṃ [M 0.0025] care dhīro,

Asakkonto sakaṃ care;

Tathāpica asakkonto,

Pāpāttānaṃ viyojaye.

The wise person should act for his own and others' welfare. If unable to do so, he should act for his own. If unable even to do that, he should keep himself from evil.

56.

56.

Bālecummattake bhūpe,

Gurumātāpitūsvapi;

Nadosaṃ kariyā pañño,

Saṅgheca jeṭṭhabhātari.

A wise person should commit no fault towards a fool, a madman, a king, a teacher, mother, or father, nor towards the Sangha or an elder brother.

57.

57.

Atthanāsaṃ [M 0.0026] manotāpaṃ,

Ghareduccaritānica;

Vañcanaṃ avamānañca,

Matimā napakāsaye.

Loss of wealth, mental anguish, misconduct in the home, deception, and humiliation—an intelligent person should not make these known.

58.

58.

Paradānaṃ janettīva,

Leṭṭuva paradabbakaṃ;

Attāva sabbabhūtāni,

Yo passati so passati.

Another's wife should be seen as a mother, another's property as a clod of earth; one who sees all beings as oneself, that one truly sees.

59.

59.

Parūpavāde padhiro,

Paravajjesvalocano;

Paṅgulo aññanārīsu,

Dussakappesvacetano.

Deaf in criticizing others, blind to the faults of others; lame towards other women, unconscious of evil intentions.

60.

60.

Sabbaṃ suṇāti sotena,

Sabbaṃ passati cakkhunā;

Nacadiṭṭhaṃ sutaṃ dhīro,

Sabbaṃ uccitumarahati.

One hears all with the ear, one sees all with the eye; but the wise person does not deem everything seen and heard worthy of acceptance.

61.

61.

Cakkhumassa yathāandho,

Sotavā padhiroyathā;

Paññāvāssa yathāmūgo,

Balavā dubbaloriva;

Atha atthesamuppanne,

Sayetha matasāyitaṃ.

Though having eyes, be as if blind; though having ears, be as if deaf; though having wisdom, be as if mute; though strong, be as if weak. Then, when the occasion arises, one should lie down as if dead.

62.

62.

Pāpamitte [M 0.0028] vivajjetvā,

Bhajeyyu ttamapuggalaṃ;

Ovādecassa tiṭṭheyya,

Patthento acalaṃsukhaṃ.

Having avoided bad companions, one should associate with the best of persons; and should abide by their counsel, aspiring to unshakeable happiness.

63.

63.

Sususā sutavaḍḍhanaṃ,

Sutaṃ paññāyavaḍḍhanaṃ;

Paññāya atthaṃ jānāti,

Ñāto attho sukhāvaho.

The desire to hear increases learning; learning increases wisdom. By wisdom, one knows the meaning; the known meaning brings happiness.

64.

64.

Pāmojja [M 0.0029] karaṇaṃ ṭhānaṃ,

Pasaṃsāvahanaṃsukhaṃ;

Phalānisaṃso bhāveti,

Vahanto porissaṃdhuraṃ.

Bearing the human burden, one cultivates its fruit and benefit, which is a cause of joy and brings praise and happiness.

65.

65.

Atisītaṃ atiuṇhaṃ,

Atisāya midaṃahu;

Iti visaṭṭha kammante,

Khaṇā accanti māṇave.

'Too cold, too hot, this is too late'—thus, with work abandoned, the moments pass by for a person.

66.

66.

Yoca [M 0.0030] sītañca uṇhañca,

Tiṇātiyo namaññati;

Karaṃ purisa kiccāni,

So sukhā navihāyati.

But one who does not consider cold and heat as more than grass, while performing a person's duties, does not fall away from happiness.

67.

67.

Yasalābhaṃ jigīsantaṃ,

Naraṃ vajjenti dūrato;

Apatthetvāna te tasmā,

Taṃmaggaṃ maggaye budho.

A person desiring fame and gain is shunned from afar; therefore, not desiring these, the wise one should seek that path.

68.

68.

Desamosajja gacchanti,

Sīhā sappurisā gajā;

Tattheva nidhanaṃ yanti,

Kākā kāpurisā migā.

Lions, noble men, and elephants abandon their country and go elsewhere; crows, cowardly men, and deer meet their destruction right there.

69.

69.

Yasmiṃdese [M 0.0031] nasammāro,

Napiti nacabandhavā;

Nacavijjāgamo koci,

Natattha vasatī vasse.

In a country where there is no respect, no affection nor relatives, and no acquisition of any knowledge, one should not make one's dwelling there.

70.

70.

Dhanavā gaṇako rājā,

Nadī vejjā imejanā;

Yattha pañca navijjante,

Na tatthavasatī vase.

A wealthy person, an astrologer, a king, a river, and a physician; where these five are not found, one should not make one's dwelling there.

71.

71.

Asanaṃ [M 0.0032] bhayamantānaṃ,

Maccānaṃ maraṇaṃ bhayaṃ;

Uttamānantusabbesaṃ,

Avamānaṃ paraṃ bhayaṃ.

For the armed, the fear is of weapons; for mortals, the fear is of death; but for the best of all, disrespect is the supreme fear.

72.

72.

Amānanā yattha siyā,

Santānaṃ avamānanā;

Hīnasamānanāvāpi,

Na tatthavasati vase.

Where there is disrespect, where the good are scorned, or where the inferior are honored, one should not make one's dwelling there.

73.

73.

Yatthālasoca dakkhoca,

Sūro bhīruca pūjito;

Na santā tattha vassanti,

Avisesakarena ko.

Where the lazy and the diligent, the brave and the cowardly are honored alike, the good do not reside there; who would, where no distinction is made?

74.

74.

Dukkho [M 0.0033] nivāso sambādhe,

Ṭhāne asucisaṅkaṭe;

Tato rimhipiyānante,

Tatoca akataññunaṃ.

Dwelling in a crowded, unclean, and perilous place is suffering; more so is the pain regarding a distant loved one, and more so dwelling with the ungrateful.

75.

75.

Siṅgīnaṃ pañcahatthena,

Satena vājinaṃ caje;

Hatthīnantu sahassena,

Desacāgena dujjane.

One should keep away from a horned animal by five cubits, a horse by a hundred, and an elephant by a thousand; but from a wicked person, by abandoning the country.

76.

76.

Caje [M 0.0034] ekaṃ kulassatthaṃ,

Gāmassatthaṃ kulaṃ caje;

Gāmaṃ janapadassatthaṃ;

Attatthaṃ pathavī caje.

One should give up an individual for the sake of the family; give up a family for the sake of the village; give up a village for the sake of the country; give up the earth for the sake of oneself.

77.

77.

Calatyekenapādena,

Tiṭṭhatyekena paṇḍito;

Asamikhyaparaṃṭhānaṃ,

Pubbamāyanaṃnacaje.

The wise person moves with one foot and stands with the other; without having examined a new place, one should not abandon the former one.

78.

78.

Ṭhānabhaṭṭhā [M 0.0035] nasobhanti,

Dantākesāna khānarā;

Matimā itiviññāya,

Saṭhānaṃ nacaje lahuṃ.

Fallen from their place, they do not shine: teeth, hair, nails, and men. The wise one, understanding this, does not quickly abandon their own place.

The friend through association with whom well-being increases—the wise person should treat him as himself in all affairs.

Even a quality equal to omniscience languishes without support; a priceless jewel, too, shines only by its setting in gold.

As sugarcane is progressively sweeter joint by joint towards the tip, so is a good friend; a wicked person is the opposite.

By relying on a good friend, all those qualities come to be for living beings. Therefore, one should always show reverence towards good friends.

The person from whom one might understand the Dhamma, the virtuous one who dispels one's doubt—he is indeed a lamp and a refuge. A wise person should not forsake such a friend.

The farmer, the merchant, the minister, the ascetic, and the one learned and virtuous; when these are prosperous, the kingdom too will be prosperous.

When these become weak, the kingdom too becomes weak. Therefore, one who desires a strong kingdom should maintain its prosperity.

The warrior king who rules the country unjustly becomes an adversary to all his own people.

When the arrogant king causes harm in the great and fertile fields and to those who have striven in battle, he comes into conflict with his army.

Likewise, by harming the sages who are restrained and lead the holy life, the unjust warrior king comes into conflict with heaven.

Great rivers are self-formed, not made by another, and swift in their course; so too is the sovereignty of a powerful king in his own kingdom.

Whether it be evil or good, whatever a leader does, the world acts in that very way; this a wise person should understand.

As female turtles, fish, hens, and cows care for their young, so too should kings among men.

Without a leader, they perish; with too many leaders, they perish; with a female leader, they perish; with a young leader, they perish.

Where there are many leaders, all considering themselves wise, all desiring greatness, their work is ruined.

Having many leaders leads not to prosperity but greatly to ruin; they are crushed and destroyed, like lotuses by seven elephants.

A discontented ascetic is lost; a contented king is lost; a modest courtesan is lost; and a shameless woman of good family is lost.

One should not go before the group; when the work is accomplished, the fruit is equal. But if there is failure in the work, the outspoken one is struck down there.

A resolute ascetic, a hero wounded in battle, a drunkard, and a visiting king—these I do not trust.

One should know a servant by their task, relatives when fear has come, a friend in adversity, and a wife in the loss of wealth.

One should praise a hero returned from battle, wealth brought home, aged grain, and a wife whose youth has passed.

Among the good who are faithful and loving, one does not count a hundred coins; among the faithless and unloving, one counts even a hundred coins as much.

Giving is the taming of the untamed; giving accomplishes every aim. By giving and by pleasant speech, they rise up and they bow down.

The giver of food in a famine, the giver of gold in a time of plenty, and the giving of safety in a time of fear—this is highly esteemed even in heaven.

A leader always has a hundred eyes and a hundred ears for reports; nevertheless, he is as one blind and deaf. Such is the nature of a leader.

Patience, vigilance, exertion, sharing, and compassion—these are the virtues of a leader, to be desired by one who seeks his own welfare.

One who is gentle is despised; one who is too harsh incurs enmity. Knowing both these, one should practice the middle way.

Attaṃ mahante ṭhapetuṃ,

It is not possible by extreme gentleness, nor by extreme harshness, to establish oneself in greatness; therefore, one should practice both.

One should attend to the sun from one's back, and to a fire from one's front; serve a master with one's whole being, and the next world without deceit.

One should not serve a harsh master, nor should one serve a miserly one; then one should serve one who promotes, and then one who restrains.

The protection of learning is constant application; of a family, tradition; of a person, knowledge. A king's heedlessness leads to ruin. But for women, I know no protection at all.

Itthikathā

A Discourse on Women

198.

Namātarā [M 0.0089] dhītuyā vāpi,

Bhagīniyā vicakkhaṇo;

Navivittāsane mante,

Nārī māyāvinīnanu.

A wise man should not confer in a secluded seat with his mother, daughter, or sister; for indeed, women are full of wiles.

Vijjutānañca lolattaṃ,

Satthānañcatitikkhaṇaṃ;

Sīghataṃ vāyutejānaṃ,

Anukubbanti nāriyo.

Women imitate the fickleness of lightning, the great sharpness of weapons, and the swiftness of wind and fire.

Buddhicāpi catugguṇo;

Chaguṇo hoti vāyāmo,

Kāmotva ṭṭha guṇobhave.

A woman's appetite is twofold, her intelligence fourfold, her effort is sixfold, and her sensual desire is eightfold.

Sūrāca balavantoca,

Sabbākāmarasāharā;

Kareyya navame chandaṃ,

Unnattā hi napūrati.

If a single woman were to have eight husbands—brave, strong, and bringing all kinds of pleasures—she would still form a desire for a ninth, for due to her deficiency, she is never filled.

202.

202.

Lapanti [M 0.0090] saddhimaññena,

Passantaññaṃ savibbhamā;

Cittakaṃ cintayantaññaṃ,

Nārīnaṃnāma ko piyo.

They speak with one man, look coquettishly at another, and in their heart think of yet another. Who, indeed, is dear to women?

203.

203.

Jivhāsahassiko yo hi,

Jīve vassasataṃ naro;

Tena nikammuno vutto,

Thīdoso kiṃkhayaṃgato.

If a man with a thousand tongues were to live for a hundred years, how could the faults of women, spoken of by him, be exhausted?

204.

204.

Aggi āpo thiyo muḷho,

Sappo rājakulānica;

Payatanāpagantabbo,

Maccupāṇaharāniti.

Fire, water, women, a fool, a snake, and royal families—these should be carefully avoided, for they are takers of life.

205.

205.

Itthīnaṃ [M 0.0091] dujjanā nañca,

Visāsonopapajjate;

Vīsassa siṅgino roga,

Nadīrājakulassaca.

Trust should not be placed in women, wicked people, poison, a horned animal, disease, a river, or a royal family.

206.

206.

Satthaṃ sunicchitamatīpi vicintanīyaṃ,

Svārādhitopya vanipo parisaṅkanīyo;

Hatthaṅgatāpi yuvatīparirakkhanīyā,

Satthāvanīpayuvatīsu kutovasitthaṃ.

A weapon, though well-assessed, should be carefully considered; a merchant, though well-propitiated, is to be suspected; a young woman, though in hand, is to be guarded. In weapons, merchants, and young women, whence comes reliability?

207.

Ayuttakammāraddhanaṃ virodho,

Saṅghassa yuddhañca mahābalehi;

Vissāsakammaṃ pamādāsu niccaṃ,

Dvārānimaccussa vadanti paññā.

Undertaking improper actions, the conflict of a group with the mighty, and always placing trust in the negligent—these the wise declare to be the doors to death.

208.

208.

Vātaṃ jālena naro parāmase,

Osiñce sāgaraṃ ekapāṇinā;

Sakenatālena janeyya ghosaṃ,

Yo sabbabhāvaṃ pamadāsu osaje.

He who would entrust his entire being to women might as well try to grasp the wind with a net, bail out the ocean with a single hand, or produce a sound with his own palm.

209.

Itthīpi [M 0.0093] hi ekacciyā,

Seyyā vuttāva muninā;

Bhaṇḍāna muttamaṃ itthī,

Aggupaṭṭhāyikātica.

Indeed, a certain woman is said by the Sage to be superior: 'A woman is the best of possessions, and a chief supporter.'

210.

Niccaṃ ātāpi ussuko;

Sabbakāmaharaṃ posaṃ,

Bhattāraṃ nātimaññati.

She does not despise her husband—the man who brings all she desires, who always supports her, ever ardent and zealous.

211.

Issācārena rosaye;

Bhattuca garunosabbe,

Paṭipūjeti paṇḍito.

Nor should she anger her husband with jealous conduct. A wise woman honors all of her husband's venerable relatives.

212.

Uṭṭhāyikā anālāsā,

Saṅgahitaparijjanā;

Bhattu manāpaṃ carati,

Bhattakaṃ anurakkhati.

Rising early, not indolent, with her household well-managed, she acts in a way that is pleasing to her husband and guards his property.

213.

Bhattuchandavasānugā;

Manāpānāma tedevā,

Yattha sā upapajjati.

The woman who thus behaves, following her husband's will, is reborn among those gods named the 'Mind-pleasing,' wherever she may arise.

Nārīrūpaṃ patibbataṃ;

Vijjā rūpa marūpānaṃ,

Khamā rūpaṃ tapassīnaṃ.

The voice is the beauty of cuckoos; devotion to her husband is the beauty of a woman; knowledge is the beauty of the ill-favored; patience is the beauty of ascetics.

Hīnāyapi surūpāya,

Vivāhaṃsadisaṃ kare.

A wise man should marry a maiden from a good family, even if she is unattractive; he should not make a marriage with a beautiful woman of low birth, for it would be an unequal match.

Nicamhāpyuttamāvijjā,

Ratanitthīpidukulā.

One should take nectar from poison, gold even from an impure substance, supreme knowledge even from one of low birth, and a jewel of a woman even from a lowly family.

Nocchiṭṭhamupajāyate.

A young woman, the mouth of a fly, a sage's water pot, a white mango, and betel leaf—these are never considered defiled.

Āyukkhayakaraṃ niccaṃ,

The morning sun, the smoke of leaves, an old woman, the water of a puddle, and eating curd at night—these things always cause the shortening of the lifespan.

One should not associate with women excessively; one should not eat food that is sweet but unwholesome; one should honor and respect the elders; one should not attend upon a teacher with deceit.

Sambhavo pema mākhyāti,

Deha mākhyātibhojanaṃ.

Conduct reveals lineage, speech reveals region, association reveals affection, and food reveals the body.

Sajja niyanti dhī kitti,

Hirī sirī matīpica.

Through the door of the utterance 'Give!', the five deities residing in the body—wisdom, fame, shame, prosperity, and intelligence—immediately depart.

Vākyaṃdehītinatthīti,

Mābhaveyya bhavebhave.

The utterance 'Give!' is painful; the utterance 'There is not!' is likewise. May the words 'Give!' and 'There is not!' never be in any future existence.

Dehīti pacchimājanā;

Passa vatthumadānassa,

Mābhavatūti īdiso.

Future generations, being enlightened, do not beg saying, 'Give!' Behold the consequence of not giving! May you not become like that.

Niguṇe guṇavāpiha;

Aṭṭhānaṭṭheyyabhāvena,

Gajindoiva dabbake.

A great one becomes very small, and even a virtuous one becomes worthless here among the worthless, by being in an unsuitable place, like a lordly elephant in a confined space.

Khuddopyatimahā bhave;

Hemapabbatamāpajja,

Sovaṇṇākira pakkhino.

By relying on a great one, even a small person may become very great; just as birds, having reached a golden mountain, indeed become golden.

Ekibhāvo hidujjayo;

Tiṇena vaṭṭate rajju,

The unity of many who are weak is indeed difficult to conquer; a rope is twisted from blades of grass, and by it even an elephant is bound.

Tejasi kiṃ karissati;

Nivātasaṇṭhito aggi,

Sayamevūpasammati.

Even if capable, what can a powerful person accomplish without companions? A fire situated in a place without wind is extinguished by itself.

228.

Bhūpādīhi katodaṇḍo,

Sakkāhoti na bhaccajo.

The splendor born of austerity is forbearance, which is achievable and not born of praise. The punishment inflicted by kings is bearable, but not that which is inflicted by servants.

Maṃsabhakkhe kutodayā;

Surāpāṇe kutosaccaṃ,

Pakodhamhi kutotapo.

Whence purity in association with women? Whence compassion in a meat-eater? Whence truth in a drinker of liquor? Whence austerity in one who is wrathful?

Amittassaca paṇḍito;

Yocā missena saṃhīro,

Hadayattheno yonaro.

A wise man should not tell a secret to a woman or to an enemy; and the man who is intimate with an enemy is treacherous at heart.

Mantabhedabhayā tassa,

Dāsabhūto titikkhati.

The man who reveals a secret matter must endure the other, having become his slave, out of fear that the confidence will be broken.

Tamheva cāgate kāle,

Bhinde ghaṭami vuppale.

One should carry an enemy on one's shoulder, so long as the time has not yet come. But when that very time has arrived, one should shatter him like a pot upon a rock.

Mitaṃ dhaññaṃ nidhāpeyya,

One should go frequently to the threshing floor, the hall, the cattle, and the field; one should store a measured amount of grain, and one should cook a measured amount at home.

Soṇṭhaṃniddhālukaṃ makkhaṃ,

The wise one should abandon anger, greed, intoxication, pride, sloth, deceit, negligence, stubbornness, drowsiness, denigration, and stinginess.

Vatthālaṅkārapuṇṇāyaṃ,

Anger, born within, surely destroys the angry one, just as fire destroys a chest full of clothes and ornaments.

If anger arises, one should reflect on the simile of the saw; if craving for tastes arises, one should remember the simile of the son's flesh.

Denigration smears a virtue as if it were a fault; when in a quarrel with another, it proclaims even the slightest flaw to be a great fault.

Kuddha mapaṭikujjhanto,

Saṅgāmaṃ jeti dujjayaṃ.

He who gets angry in return at one who is angry is thereby the more evil. By not getting angry in return at one who is angry, one wins a battle hard to win.

Rāhu vijjāsasaṅkassa,

Tapodhanahutāsano.

Lust, indeed, is a dart of the mind, a destroyer of the highest good of virtue; it is Rāhu for the moon of knowledge, a fire for the wealth of asceticism.

Negligence is born of intoxication; from negligence, decline is born; from decline, corruptions arise. Why would a wise one not abandon intoxication?

Fruit-laden trees bend down; wise people bend; but dry wood and a fool break, they do not bend.

One who respects elders should give them place. Surely, even a lifeless palm tree bends towards those nearby.

He who constantly maintains proper conduct towards those worthy of respect, having abandoned baseness, becomes established in the highest state.

Where people do not know a person, either by birth or by conduct, one should not display pride while living among such unknown folk.

When living unknown, even one who is like a blazing fire—a person of wisdom—should endure even the rebuke of a servant.

In matters of wealth, grain, and expenditure, likewise in the acquisition of knowledge, and in dealing with messengers and in business—one should always be without shame.

For when a task is completed, no one properly regards the doer; therefore, one should have all undertakings left with something remaining.

The remainder of a debt, the remainder of a fire, and the remainder of an enemy—these three grow again and again; therefore, one should leave no remainder.

There is no wealth equal to knowledge, no enemy equal to disease, no love equal to love for oneself, and no power greater than kamma.

One creates one's own fortune, and one's own misfortune too; for another does not create fortune or misfortune for another.

A king himself should know his income and expenditure; he himself should know what is done and what is not done. By oneself one should see one's own becoming, and the deeds done and not done.

Kaṇṭakena kaṇṭakaṃ.

One should extract help by means of a friend, an enemy by means of an enemy, a splinter stuck in the foot by means of a tool, and a thorn by means of a thorn.

One should bow to him who bows; one should associate with him who associates; one should do a service for him who does a service. One should not do a good turn for someone who does not desire one's welfare; one should not associate with him who does not associate.

Apetacittena nasambhajeyya;

Dijo dumaṃ khīṇaphala mañatvā,

Aññaṃ sapekkheyya mahāti loko.

One should abandon him who abandons and form new friendships; one should not associate with a person of estranged mind. A bird, knowing a tree to be devoid of fruit, should seek another, for the world is vast.
